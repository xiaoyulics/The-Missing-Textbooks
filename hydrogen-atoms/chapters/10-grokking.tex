\casechapter{Grokking}{Modular addition and its Fourier circuit}{Modular addition, quadratic two-layer network}{ch:grokking}
\begin{refsection}[chapters/10-grokking.bib]

\begin{chaptersummary}
Networks trained on part of the addition table of $\Z_p$ fit their training data early and predict the rest of the table much later: this is grokking. In a two-layer network with quadratic activation the generalizing solution can be written down: each neuron carries one Fourier frequency, and trigonometric identities turn the neurons into clocks $\cos(\omega(a+b-c))$ that peak at the correct answer. We prove that margin maximization forces this circuit (single-frequency neurons whose readout phase is the sum of their input phases, every frequency present, at least six neurons per frequency), and that on a diagonal linear network weight decay acts exactly as an initialization scale shrinking like $e^{-\lambda t}$, which predicts a delay $\lambda^{-1}\log(\alpha/\alpha_c)$ that simulations confirm. The model is silent on transformers that use few frequencies or another algorithm, on grokking without weight decay, and on networks far from the margin limit.
\end{chaptersummary}

\section{The phenomenon: memorize now, generalize later}\label{sec:grokking:phenomenon}

In 2021 a group at OpenAI trained small transformers on tables of binary operations: addition, subtraction and division modulo $97$, polynomials such as $x^2+xy+y^2$, and composition of permutations in $S_5$. Each network saw a random part of a table and had to fill in the rest \parencite{PowerEtAl2022}.\sidenote{The work was presented at an ICLR 2021 workshop on mathematical reasoning and posted to arXiv in January 2022. The verb \emph{grok}, to understand something completely, comes from Robert Heinlein's novel \emph{Stranger in a Strange Land} (1961).} The networks were two-layer transformers of width $128$ with about $4\times10^5$ parameters, trained with AdamW at learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $1$. On division modulo $97$ with half of the table for training, training accuracy was nearly perfect after about $10^3$ steps, while validation accuracy stayed near chance and approached $100\%$ only after close to $10^6$ steps. Power and coauthors called this \term{grokking}\index{grokking}. The delay grew steeply as the training set shrank: at $25$ to $30\%$ of the table, removing $1\%$ of the data raised the median time to generalization by $40$ to $50\%$, and some operations never generalized. Of the interventions they tried, weight decay improved data efficiency the most.

\begin{observation}[\textcite{PowerEtAl2022}; \textcite{NandaEtAl2023}]\label[observation]{obs:grokking:delay}
A small network trained with AdamW and weight decay on a random fraction of the addition table of $\Z_p$ (between about $25\%$ and $50\%$, for $p$ near $100$) reaches $100\%$ training accuracy early, stays near chance on the rest of the table for one to three orders of magnitude longer, and then reaches about $100\%$ test accuracy within a comparatively short interval. The delay grows steeply as the training fraction decreases, and weight decay shortens it.
\end{observation}

What does the network compute once it generalizes? \Textcite{NandaEtAl2023} reverse-engineered a one-layer transformer trained on $30\%$ of the addition table modulo $113$. Its embedding of a number $a$ is concentrated on five ``key frequencies'' $\omega_k=2\pi k/113$, $k\in\{14,35,41,42,52\}$, as $\cos(\omega_ka)$ and $\sin(\omega_ka)$. Attention and the MLP combine these into $\cos(\omega_k(a+b))$ and $\sin(\omega_k(a+b))$ by the addition formulas, and the unembedding produces logits close to $\sum_k\cos(\omega_k(a+b-c))$, largest at $c=a+b$. Progress measures built from these Fourier components showed three phases: memorization (epochs $0$ to $1.4$ thousand); circuit formation (to $9.4$ thousand), while the test loss stays flat; and cleanup (to $14$ thousand), when weight decay removes the memorizing components and the test loss drops. The jump in test accuracy ends a gradual process, the kind of hidden progress met in \cref{ch:parity}.

Grokking on group operations is reproducible across transformers and multilayer perceptrons. \Textcite{LiuEtAl2022} mapped phase diagrams over learning rate and weight decay with four phases (comprehension, grokking, memorization, confusion). \Textcite{LiuEtAl2023} induced grokking on MNIST, with $1000$ training images and an enlarged initialization, and argued that weight decay $\lambda$ shrinks the weight norm like $e^{-\lambda t}$, so that generalization waits until the norm reaches a critical value, after a time of about $\lambda^{-1}\log(w_0/w_c)$; on modular addition, constraining the weight norm to a smaller value largely removed the delay. Delayed generalization has also been reported for Gaussian processes and linear regression \parencite{MillerEtAl2024}, and a delayed onset of adversarial robustness for convolutional networks on CIFAR-10 \parencite{HumayunEtAl2024}. Grokking is thus a property of a training configuration (initialization scale, weight decay, data fraction) as much as of a task, and on group operations the generalizing networks compute with characters of the group.

Two questions follow. \emph{Which} computation does a generalizing network perform, and why that one among the many functions that fit the training data? And \emph{why so late}: what sets the delay between fitting the training set and generalizing?

\section{The model: a quadratic network on the addition table}\label{sec:grokking:model}

In this chapter $p\ge3$ is an odd prime, the modulus; this overrides, locally, the global use of $p$ for a number of features. The elements of $\grokZp=\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}$ are written $a$, $b$, $c$, and arithmetic on them is modulo $p$.\sidenote{The proofs use only that $p$ is odd. Primality matters elsewhere: for composite $p$, a frequency that shares a factor with $p$ cannot separate all the classes on its own.}

\paragraph{The task.} The data are the $p^2$ correct triples $(a,b,a+b)$. A training set is a random set of pairs $(a,b)$, labeled by $a+b$; the test error is the error on the remaining pairs. The input is one-hot, $x=(e_a,e_b)\in\R^{2p}$, and says nothing about the arithmetic.

\paragraph{The network.} A two-layer network with $m$ hidden neurons, quadratic activation, and $p$ outputs, the logits. The first-layer weight vector $w_r\in\R^{2p}$ of neuron $r$ splits into two functions $u_r,u'_r\colon\grokZp\to\R$, so that $\ip{w_r,x}=u_r(a)+u'_r(b)$, and its readout is a third function $v_r\colon\grokZp\to\R$. The logit of class $c$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:net}
  f_c(a,b;\theta)=\sum_{r=1}^m v_r(c)\bigl(u_r(a)+u'_r(b)\bigr)^2 .
\end{equation}
The parameters are $\theta=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_m)$ with $\theta_r=(u_r,u'_r,v_r)\in(\R^p)^3$; we write $\norm{u}^2=\sum_au(a)^2$ and $\norm{\theta_r}^2=\norm{u_r}^2+\norm{u'_r}^2+\norm{v_r}^2$. The network predicts the class with the largest logit. Training minimizes the cross-entropy over the training set plus the weight decay $\frac\lambda2\norm\theta^2$, by gradient flow in the analysis and by AdamW in the experiments.

\paragraph{Two structural facts.} The network is \term{homogeneous of degree three} neuron by neuron\index{homogeneous network}: replacing $\theta_r$ by $t\theta_r$ with $t>0$ multiplies the contribution of neuron $r$ to every logit by $t^3$. Homogeneity makes margins the right currency, as in \cref{ch:margin}. And the task is invariant under translations: if $(a,b,c)$ is a correct triple, then so is $(a+g,b+h,c+g+h)$ for all $g,h\in\grokZp$. The characters $a\mapsto e^{2\pi\grokii ka/p}$ of $\grokZp$ diagonalize this symmetry, so we expect Fourier analysis.

\paragraph{What was thrown away.} Attention, ReLU, normalization and Adam. One-hot inputs with free first-layer weights are a learned embedding, and the quadratic activation is the simplest nonlinearity that multiplies, $(x+y)^2=x^2+2xy+y^2$; it makes every logit a polynomial in the weights, so the Fourier analysis is exact. The margin analysis uses the full table. For the delay we use the diagonal linear network of \cref{ch:diagonal} with weight decay, in which the passage from the lazy to the rich regime can be computed.

\begin{dictionary}
one-hot $(e_a,e_b)$; first-layer functions $u_r,u'_r$ & token embeddings of $a$ and $b$, read by an MLP neuron\\
quadratic activation; the cross term $2u_r(a)u'_r(b)$ & the multiplication done by attention and by the MLP nonlinearity\\
frequency $\zeta$ and phases $\varphi,\psi$ of a Fourier neuron & a key frequency, and the cosine and sine directions of the embedding \parencite{NandaEtAl2023}\\
readout $v_r(c)\propto\cos(\omega_\zeta c+\varphi+\psi)$ & rows of the unembedding; logits $\propto\sum_\zeta\cos(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c))$\\
normalized margin, norm $\norm{\theta}_{2,3}$ & the long-time limit of cross-entropy training with small weight decay\\
initialization scale $\alpha$; effective scale $\alpha e^{-\lambda t}$ & parameter norm, decaying under weight decay after memorization\\
critical scale $\alpha_c$ & the norm below which training leaves the lazy regime\\
\end{dictionary}

\paragraph{What one had to notice.} That generalizing on part of a group's table means finding the group structure, carried for a cyclic group by characters; that one multiplication suffices, since $\cos(\omega(a+b))$ is a sum of products of a function of $a$ and a function of $b$; and that training selects among the networks that fit by a norm, so ``which circuit?'' becomes an extremal problem that the same characters diagonalize.

\section{The solution: a Fourier circuit for addition}\label{sec:grokking:circuit}

\paragraph{Fourier analysis on $\grokZp$.} For $u\colon\grokZp\to\R$ let $\hat u(k)=\sum_{a\in\grokZp}u(a)e^{-2\pi\grokii ka/p}$ for $k\in\grokZp$. In this normalization inversion, Plancherel's identity, and the symmetry of real functions read
\[
  u(a)=\frac1p\sum_{k\in\grokZp}\hat u(k)e^{2\pi\grokii ka/p},\qquad \sum_au(a)^2=\frac1p\sum_k\abs{\hat u(k)}^2,\qquad \hat u(-k)=\overline{\hat u(k)}
\]
(\cref{thm:toolkit:fourier-zp}). All three follow from the orthogonality relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:orth}
  \sum_{a\in\grokZp}e^{2\pi\grokii qa/p}=p\,\ind\{q\equiv0\pmod p\}\qquad\text{for every integer }q .
\end{equation}
Because $p$ is odd, the nonzero frequencies pair up as $\pm\zeta$ with $\zeta\in\{1,\dots,\frac{p-1}2\}$; we write $\omega_\zeta=2\pi\zeta/p$. A real function whose transform vanishes outside $\pm\zeta$ is $a\mapsto\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta a+\varphi)$ with $\rho=\frac2p\abs{\hat u(\zeta)}$ and $\varphi=\arg\hat u(\zeta)$.

\begin{lemma}[Sum of cosines]\label[lemma]{lem:grokking:cosines}
For every integer $q$, $\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=1}^{(p-1)/2}\cos\frac{2\pi\zeta q}{p}=\frac{p\,\ind\{q\equiv0\}-1}{2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In \eqref{eq:grokking:orth} the term $a=0$ equals $1$, and the terms $a=\zeta$ and $a=p-\zeta$ are complex conjugates with real part $\cos(2\pi\zeta q/p)$. Hence \eqref{eq:grokking:orth} reads $1+2\sum_{\zeta=1}^{(p-1)/2}\cos(2\pi\zeta q/p)=p\,\ind\{q\equiv0\}$.
\end{proof}

With $q=a+b-c$ the lemma says that the logits
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:target}
  f_c(a,b)=\sum_{\zeta=1}^{(p-1)/2}\cos\bigl(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c)\bigr)=\frac{p\,\ind\{c=a+b\}-1}{2}
\end{equation}
are $\frac{p-1}2$ on the correct class and $-\frac12$ on every other class. Each term is a \term{clock}\index{clock}: $\cos(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c))$ is largest when the angle $\omega_\zeta c$ equals $\omega_\zeta a+\omega_\zeta b$, so it compares the sum of two rotations with a third. One clock already peaks only at the correct class, because $p$ is prime; the sum of all of them makes every wrong class tie. How does a sum of squares of first-layer activations produce one clock?

\paragraph{One frequency at a time.} Fix a frequency $\zeta$ and write $\omega=\omega_\zeta$. Call the neuron with $u(a)=\cos(\omega a+\varphi)$, $u'(b)=\cos(\omega b+\psi)$ and $v(c)=\cos(\omega c+\varphi+\psi)$ the \term{Fourier neuron}\index{Fourier neuron} of frequency $\zeta$ and phases $(\varphi,\psi)$, and write
\[
  n_{\varphi,\psi}(a,b,c)=\bigl(\cos(\omega a+\varphi)+\cos(\omega b+\psi)\bigr)^2\cos(\omega c+\varphi+\psi)
\]
for its contribution to the logits. Its readout phase is the sum of its input phases; \cref{thm:grokking:neuron} will explain why. We use the readout phase $\chi=\varphi+\psi$ and the difference $\delta=\varphi-\psi$ as coordinates; the function $n_{\varphi,\psi}$ depends on $(\varphi,\psi)$ only through $\chi$ and $\delta$ modulo $2\pi$.

\begin{lemma}[Cancellation within one frequency]\label[lemma]{lem:grokking:block}
Let $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_K$ be real, let $(\varphi_r,\psi_r)$ be phases, and let $\chi_r=\varphi_r+\psi_r$ and $\delta_r=\varphi_r-\psi_r$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:block}
  \sum_{r=1}^K\beta_r\,n_{\varphi_r,\psi_r}(a,b,c)=\frac12\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^K\beta_r\Bigr)\cos\bigl(\omega(a+b-c)\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }a,b,c\in\grokZp
\end{equation}
if and only if, for all integers $j$ and $s$ with $\abs j\le2$, $\abs s\le1$ and $(j,s)\ne(0,0)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:moments}
  M_{j,s}\defeq\sum_{r=1}^K\beta_re^{\grokii(j\chi_r+s\delta_r)}=0 .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The plan is to expand one neuron into cosines of linear forms in $(a,b,c)$ and to read off the coefficient of each. Put $A=\omega a+\varphi$, $B=\omega b+\psi$ and $C=\omega c+\chi$. Using $\cos^2x=\frac12(1+\cos2x)$ and $4\cos A\cos B\cos C=\cos(A+B-C)+\cos(A+B+C)+\cos(A-B+C)+\cos(A-B-C)$,
\begin{align*}
  n_{\varphi,\psi}&=\tfrac12\cos(A+B-C)+\cos C+\tfrac14\bigl[\cos(2A\pm C)+\cos(2B\pm C)\bigr]\\
  &\qquad+\tfrac12\bigl[\cos(A+B+C)+\cos(A-B\pm C)\bigr],
\end{align*}
each $\pm$ standing for the sum of the two terms. Because $\chi=\varphi+\psi$, the first term is $\frac12\cos(\omega(a+b-c))$ for every neuron. Every other term is $\cos(\omega\ell+j\chi+s\delta)$ with a linear form $\ell$ in $(a,b,c)$ and integers $(j,s)$: $C$ has $(\ell;j,s)=(c;1,0)$; $2A+C$ has $(2a+c;2,1)$; $2A-C$ has $(2a-c;0,1)$; $2B+C$ has $(2b+c;2,-1)$; $2B-C$ has $(2b-c;0,-1)$; $A+B+C$ has $(a+b+c;2,0)$; $A-B+C$ has $(a-b+c;1,1)$; and $A-B-C$ has $(a-b-c;-1,1)$. Up to sign, these pairs $(j,s)$ are the seven nonzero pairs with $\abs j\le2$ and $\abs s\le1$. Weighting by $\beta_r$ and summing over $r$, the term with $(\ell;j,s)$ contributes its coefficient times $\operatorname{Re}\bigl(e^{\grokii\omega\ell}M_{j,s}\bigr)$, and $M_{-j,-s}=\overline{M_{j,s}}$ because the weights are real. If \eqref{eq:grokking:moments} holds, then every such contribution vanishes, and \eqref{eq:grokking:block} follows.

Conversely, the eighteen functions $e^{\pm\grokii\omega(a+b-c)}$ and $e^{\pm\grokii\omega\ell}$ are characters of $\grokZp^3$ with distinct frequency vectors: two of these vectors differ in some coordinate by $1$, $2$ or $4$, which multiplied by $\zeta$ is not divisible by $p$. Characters with distinct frequency vectors are orthogonal by \eqref{eq:grokking:orth}. So if \eqref{eq:grokking:block} holds, then the coefficient of each $e^{\grokii\omega\ell}$ on its left side vanishes, and that coefficient is a nonzero multiple of the corresponding $M_{j,s}$.
\end{proof}

In the language of numerical integration, \eqref{eq:grokking:moments} says that the points $(\chi_r,\delta_r)$ with weights $\beta_r$ integrate every trigonometric polynomial of degree at most $2$ in $\chi$ and at most $1$ in $\delta$ exactly, as the uniform measure of total mass $\sum_r\beta_r$ does. Such weighted points form a \term{cubature rule}\index{cubature rule} on the torus, and every cubature rule of this kind gives a circuit.

\begin{theorem}[A Fourier network for modular addition]\label{thm:grokking:fourier}
For each frequency $\zeta\in\{1,\dots,\frac{p-1}2\}$ and each pair of phases $(\varphi,\psi)\in\{0,\frac{2\pi}3,\frac{4\pi}3\}^2$, take the neuron with $u(a)=\cos(\omega_\zeta a+\varphi)$, $u'(b)=\cos(\omega_\zeta b+\psi)$ and $v(c)=\frac29\cos(\omega_\zeta c+\varphi+\psi)$. The resulting network of width $\frac92(p-1)$ has logits
\[
  f_c(a,b)=\frac{p\,\ind\{c=a+b\}-1}2\qquad\text{for all }a,b,c\in\grokZp .
\]
In particular it classifies the whole table correctly, and every margin $f_{a+b}(a,b)-f_c(a,b)$ with $c\ne a+b$ equals $\frac p2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\zeta$. Its nine neurons are the Fourier neurons $n_{\varphi,\psi}$ with weights $\beta=\frac29$. Writing $j\chi+s\delta=(j+s)\varphi+(j-s)\psi$, the moments factorize over the grid,
\[
  M_{j,s}=\frac29\Bigl(\sum_{\varphi}e^{\grokii(j+s)\varphi}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{\psi}e^{\grokii(j-s)\psi}\Bigr),
\]
and by \eqref{eq:grokking:orth} with modulus $3$ the first factor vanishes unless $3$ divides $j+s$, the second unless $3$ divides $j-s$. If $3$ divides both, then it divides $2j$ and $2s$, hence $j$ and $s$, and $\abs j\le2$, $\abs s\le1$ force $j=s=0$. So \eqref{eq:grokking:moments} holds, and \cref{lem:grokking:block} shows that the nine neurons of frequency $\zeta$ contribute $\frac12\cdot9\cdot\frac29\cos(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c))=\cos(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c))$. Summing over $\zeta$ gives \eqref{eq:grokking:target}.
\end{proof}

Three phases per input turn every unwanted term into a sum of three unit vectors spaced by $2\pi/3$. We checked the identity numerically for every odd $p\le23$.

\begin{remark}[Other designs]\label[remark]{rem:grokking:designs}
A pair of Fourier neurons with phases $(\varphi,\psi)$ and $(\varphi,\psi+\pi)$ turns squares into a product, $n_{\varphi,\psi}+n_{\varphi,\psi+\pi}=\bigl[(x+y)^2-(x-y)^2\bigr]z=4xyz$, with $x=\cos(\omega a+\varphi)$, $y=\cos(\omega b+\psi)$ and $z=\cos(\omega c+\varphi+\psi)$. \Textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024} use four such pairs, one for each product in $\cos(A+B-C)=\cos A\cos B\cos C-\sin A\sin B\cos C+\sin A\cos B\sin C+\cos A\sin B\sin C$: eight neurons per frequency. Three pairs suffice. For $\theta\in\{0,\frac\pi3,\frac{2\pi}3\}$ take $(\varphi,\psi)=(\theta,\theta)$ and $(\theta,\theta+\pi)$; expanding $4xyz$, the three unwanted terms carry the phases $4\theta$, $2\theta$ and $-2\theta$, whose exponentials sum to zero over $\theta$, so
\begin{align*}
  \frac13\sum_\theta\bigl(n_{\theta,\theta}+n_{\theta,\theta+\pi}\bigr)&=\frac43\sum_\theta\cos(\omega a+\theta)\cos(\omega b+\theta)\cos(\omega c+2\theta)\\
  &=\cos\bigl(\omega(a+b-c)\bigr).
\end{align*}
These six neurons per frequency give width $3(p-1)$; small blocks of this kind are the building material of \textcite{Tian2025}, who composes global minimizers of a squared loss from them. \Textcite{Gromov2023} uses independent uniformly random phases with $\chi=\varphi+\psi$ and $N$ neurons per frequency; with readout weight $2/N$ in our normalization, each $M_{j,s}$ is then of size about $2/\sqrt N$, and the unwanted terms cancel only as the width grows.
\end{remark}

The six-neuron identity writes $\cos(A+B-C)=\operatorname{Re}(e^{\grokii A}e^{\grokii B}e^{-\grokii C})$ with three products, as few as multiplying two complex numbers needs.\sidenote{Gauss's trick multiplies $x_1+\grokii x_2$ by $y_1+\grokii y_2$ with the three real products $x_1y_1$, $x_2y_2$ and $(x_1+x_2)(y_1+y_2)$; over the reals three is the minimum.} Can a frequency do with fewer than six Fourier neurons? Not with positive weights, the case that margins select.

\begin{lemma}[Six neurons per frequency]\label[lemma]{lem:grokking:six}
If $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_K>0$ and the moment conditions \eqref{eq:grokking:moments} hold, then the points $(\chi_r,\delta_r)$, taken modulo $2\pi$, include at least six distinct points. In particular $K\ge6$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Neurons with the same point $(\chi_r,\delta_r)$ modulo $2\pi$ contribute the same summand to every moment, so merging them and adding their weights changes neither the hypothesis nor the positivity; assume that the $K$ points are distinct. Consider the six-dimensional space of trigonometric polynomials
\[
  P(\chi,\delta)=\sum_{\abs j\le1}\,\sum_{s\in\{0,1\}}c_{js}e^{\grokii(j\chi+s\delta)} .
\]
If $K<6$, then the $K$ linear conditions $P(\chi_r,\delta_r)=0$ on six unknowns have a nonzero solution $(c_{js})$. Expanding $\abs{P}^2$, every product of two of its exponentials has frequency $(j-j',s-s')$ with $\abs{j-j'}\le2$ and $\abs{s-s'}\le1$, so the moment conditions kill every term with $(j,s)\ne(j',s')$:
\[
  0=\sum_{r=1}^K\beta_r\abs{P(\chi_r,\delta_r)}^2=\sum_{j,s,j',s'}c_{js}\overline{c_{j's'}}\,M_{j-j',s-s'}=M_{0,0}\sum_{j,s}\abs{c_{js}}^2 .
\]
But $M_{0,0}=\sum_r\beta_r>0$ and the coefficients are not all zero, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

This is the classical lower bound for cubature rules with positive weights: a rule exact on all products $P\bar Q$ from a space of dimension $N$ needs $N$ points.\sidenote{The six-point rule of \cref{rem:grokking:designs} attains the bound; such a tight rule has equal weights (\cref{ex:grokking:phases}).} Without positivity the bound fails: four neurons of one frequency that are not Fourier neurons (a numerical search finds them) produce the clock up to a term depending on $c$ alone, and \textcite{Tian2025} composes exact solutions from such non-Fourier blocks. Margins exclude them.

\section{The solution: margins select the circuit}\label{sec:grokking:margin}

Why would training find this circuit? \Textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024} proposed that margin maximization alone determines it. Recall from \cref{ch:margin} that for a homogeneous network trained by cross-entropy on data it can classify, the regularized minimizers converge in direction, as the weight decay tends to zero, to a network of maximal normalized margin \parencite{WeiEtAl2019}, and gradient flow without regularization converges in direction to a Karush--Kuhn--Tucker point of the margin problem \parencite{LyuLi2020}.\sidenote{The first statement is about global minimizers, for the norm that is penalized; the second gives a stationary point of the margin problem, not necessarily a global maximizer.} Weight decay penalizes $\norm\theta^2=\sum_r\norm{\theta_r}^2$. Morwani and coauthors normalize instead by
\[
  \norm{\theta}_{2,3}=\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^m\norm{\theta_r}^3\Bigr)^{1/3},
\]
the norm matched to neurons homogeneous of degree three, with which the margin problem decouples into one problem per neuron. With the Euclidean norm it does not (\cref{ex:grokking:l2}), and we know of no complete solution then.

\begin{definition}[Margins]\label[definition]{def:grokking:margin}
The \term{margin}\index{margin!of a network} of $\theta$ at $(a,b)$ against $c\ne a+b$ is $f_{a+b}(a,b;\theta)-f_c(a,b;\theta)$. The margin $\gamma(\theta)$ of the network is the smallest of these $p^2(p-1)$ numbers, and its \term{average margin}\index{margin!average} $\grokcw(\theta)$ is their average. The \term{normalized margin} of $\theta\ne0$ is $\gamma(\theta)/\norm\theta_{2,3}^3$, which is invariant under $\theta\mapsto t\theta$ for $t>0$. A network of width $m$ is a \term{max-margin network}\index{max-margin network} if it maximizes the normalized margin among networks of width $m$.
\end{definition}

The maximum exists, because $\gamma$ is continuous on the compact set $\{\norm{\theta}_{2,3}=1\}$. The average margin is the class-weighted margin of Morwani and coauthors, the margin averaged over a uniform pair $(a,b)$ and a uniform wrong class. It is linear in the neurons: $\grokcw(\theta)=\sum_r\grokcw(\theta_r)$, the sum of the average margins of the one-neuron networks $\theta_r$.

\begin{lemma}[Average margin of one neuron]\label[lemma]{lem:grokking:cw}
For one neuron $(u,u',v)$, with $\bar v=\frac1p\sum_cv(c)$ and $(a,b)$ uniform on $\grokZp^2$,
\begin{align}
  \grokcw(u,u',v)&=\frac{2p}{p-1}\,\E_{a,b}\bigl[u(a)u'(b)\bigl(v(a+b)-\bar v\bigr)\bigr]\nonumber\\
  &=\frac{2}{(p-1)p^2}\sum_{k\ne0}\hat u(k)\,\hat u'(k)\,\overline{\hat v(k)} .\label{eq:grokking:cw}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For fixed $(a,b)$ the average of $v(c)$ over the $p-1$ wrong classes is $(p\bar v-v(a+b))/(p-1)$, so the average margin at $(a,b)$ is $\frac p{p-1}\bigl(u(a)+u'(b)\bigr)^2\bigl(v(a+b)-\bar v\bigr)$. Expand the square. The term $u(a)^2$ averages to zero, because for fixed $a$ the class $a+b$ is uniform when $b$ is, so that $\E_b[v(a+b)]=\bar v$; the term $u'(b)^2$ vanishes in the same way. The cross term $2u(a)u'(b)$ remains, which proves the first equality. For the second, insert $v(c)=\frac1p\sum_k\hat v(k)e^{2\pi\grokii kc/p}$ and sum over $a$ and $b$ separately:
\[
  \sum_{a,b}u(a)u'(b)v(a+b)=\frac1p\sum_k\hat v(k)\,\hat u(-k)\,\hat u'(-k).
\]
The term $k=0$ equals $\hat u(0)\hat u'(0)\hat v(0)/p=\sum_{a,b}u(a)u'(b)\bar v$, which the subtraction of $\bar v$ removes. Divide by $p^2$, multiply by $\frac{2p}{p-1}$, replace $k$ by $-k$, and use $\hat v(-k)=\overline{\hat v(k)}$.
\end{proof}

The squares cancel because the wrong classes are averaged uniformly: the terms $u(a)^2v(c)$ and $u'(b)^2v(c)$, which no single neuron can avoid, carry no information about $a+b$ and contribute equally to the correct logit and to the average wrong logit. What remains is a cubic form that the Fourier transform diagonalizes.

\begin{theorem}[The best single neuron]\label{thm:grokking:neuron}
Every neuron satisfies $\grokcw(\theta_r)\le\gamma^\star\norm{\theta_r}^3$, with
\[
  \gamma^\star=\sqrt{\frac2{27}}\,\frac1{\sqrt p\,(p-1)} .
\]
A neuron $\theta_r\ne0$ attains equality if and only if it is a positive multiple of a Fourier neuron: $u(a)=\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta a+\varphi)$, $u'(b)=\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta b+\psi)$ and $v(c)=\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta c+\varphi+\psi)$ for some $\rho>0$, frequency $\zeta\in\{1,\dots,\frac{p-1}2\}$, and phases $\varphi,\psi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The plan is to pair conjugate frequencies, to bound each pair by the arithmetic--geometric mean inequality, and then to use that $s\mapsto s^{3/2}$ grows faster than linearly to put all the weight on one pair. Both sides are homogeneous of degree three, so assume $\norm{\theta_r}=1$.

\emph{Pairing.} Because $u$, $u'$ and $v$ are real, the terms $k$ and $-k$ of \eqref{eq:grokking:cw} are complex conjugates, and $k\ne-k$ because $p$ is odd. With $x_\zeta=\abs{\hat u(\zeta)}$, $y_\zeta=\abs{\hat u'(\zeta)}$ and $z_\zeta=\abs{\hat v(\zeta)}$,
\[
  \grokcw(\theta_r)=\frac4{(p-1)p^2}\sum_{\zeta=1}^{(p-1)/2}\operatorname{Re}\Bigl(\hat u(\zeta)\hat u'(\zeta)\overline{\hat v(\zeta)}\Bigr)\le\frac4{(p-1)p^2}\sum_{\zeta=1}^{(p-1)/2}x_\zeta y_\zeta z_\zeta .
\]
\emph{The constraint.} Plancherel's identity, with the terms $\pm\zeta$ grouped and the term $k=0$ dropped, gives $1=\norm{u}^2+\norm{u'}^2+\norm{v}^2\ge\frac2p\sum_\zeta s_\zeta$ with $s_\zeta=x_\zeta^2+y_\zeta^2+z_\zeta^2$; equality requires $\hat u(0)=\hat u'(0)=\hat v(0)=0$.

\emph{One frequency.} By the arithmetic--geometric mean inequality $x_\zeta y_\zeta z_\zeta\le(s_\zeta/3)^{3/2}$, with equality if and only if $x_\zeta=y_\zeta=z_\zeta$. And $\sum_\zeta s_\zeta^{3/2}\le(\max_\zeta s_\zeta)^{1/2}\sum_\zeta s_\zeta\le\bigl(\sum_\zeta s_\zeta\bigr)^{3/2}$, with equality if and only if at most one $s_\zeta$ is nonzero. With $\sum_\zeta s_\zeta\le p/2$ these give
\[
  \grokcw(\theta_r)\le\frac4{(p-1)p^2}\Bigl(\frac{p}{6}\Bigr)^{3/2}=\sqrt{\frac2{27}}\,\frac1{\sqrt p\,(p-1)} .
\]
\emph{Equality.} Equality holds throughout if and only if the transforms vanish at $0$ and at every frequency except one pair $\pm\zeta$, the three moduli at $\zeta$ are equal (to $\sqrt{p/6}$), and $\hat u(\zeta)\hat u'(\zeta)\overline{\hat v(\zeta)}$ is a positive real, that is, $\arg\hat v(\zeta)=\arg\hat u(\zeta)+\arg\hat u'(\zeta)$. By the remark before \cref{lem:grokking:cosines}, these are exactly the functions $\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta a+\varphi)$, $\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta b+\psi)$ and $\rho\cos(\omega_\zeta c+\varphi+\psi)$ with $\rho=\frac2p\sqrt{p/6}=\sqrt{2/(3p)}$. Conversely, such a neuron makes every inequality of the chain an equality.
\end{proof}

The best neuron is \emph{single-frequency}, because a cubic objective under a quadratic constraint gains more from concentration than it loses; and its phases \emph{add}, so that the term $\frac12\cos(\omega(a+b)+\chi)$ of the cross term meets the readout $\cos(\omega c+\chi)$ and leaves the clock $\frac14\cos(\omega(a+b-c))$. One neuron cannot be the whole network: its logits peak at the correct class only for some pairs. The next theorem assembles optimal neurons into a network whose margins are all equal.

\begin{theorem}[Max-margin networks; \textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024}, with sharp width]\label{thm:grokking:maxmargin}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every network of width $m$ satisfies $\gamma(\theta)\le\gamma^\star\norm{\theta}_{2,3}^3$.
\item A network $\theta\ne0$ attains equality if and only if its logits are
\[
  f_c(a,b;\theta)=\gamma^\star\norm\theta_{2,3}^3\Bigl(\ind\{c=a+b\}-\frac1p\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all }a,b,c .
\]
Then every nonzero neuron is a positive multiple of a Fourier neuron, and each frequency $\zeta\in\{1,\dots,\frac{p-1}2\}$ is carried by at least six neurons.
\item Equality is attained at width $m$ if and only if $m\ge3(p-1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The plan is to compare the minimum margin with the average margin, which decomposes over neurons, and then to read the equality cases in Fourier space; part (iii) rests on the cubature lemmas of \cref{sec:grokking:circuit}.

\emph{The bound.} A minimum is at most an average, so by \cref{thm:grokking:neuron},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:chain}
  \gamma(\theta)\le\grokcw(\theta)=\sum_{r=1}^m\grokcw(\theta_r)\le\gamma^\star\sum_{r=1}^m\norm{\theta_r}^3=\gamma^\star\norm\theta_{2,3}^3 .
\end{equation}
\emph{Equality.} If the logits have the stated form, then every margin equals $\gamma^\star\norm\theta^3_{2,3}$, and equality holds. Conversely, suppose that equality holds, and write $\gamma'=\gamma(\theta)$. The first inequality in \eqref{eq:grokking:chain} is then an equality, so all margins equal $\gamma'$: for every $(a,b)$ the wrong logits share a common value $g(a,b)$, and $f_c(a,b)=g(a,b)+\gamma'\ind\{c=a+b\}$. The second inequality is an equality term by term, so by \cref{thm:grokking:neuron} every nonzero neuron is a positive multiple of a Fourier neuron. A Fourier readout has mean zero over $c$, so $\sum_cf_c(a,b)=0$, which gives $g=-\gamma'/p$.

\emph{Every frequency, six times.} Group the neurons by frequency. The neurons of frequency $\zeta$ are $\rho_r$ times Fourier neurons with phases $(\varphi_r,\psi_r)$, and they contribute $B_\zeta=\sum_r\rho_r^3\,n_{\varphi_r,\psi_r}$, whose Fourier transform in $c$, for fixed $(a,b)$, lives on $\pm\zeta$. By \cref{lem:grokking:cosines} the logits are $\gamma'(\ind\{c=a+b\}-\frac1p)=\frac{2\gamma'}p\sum_\zeta\cos(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c))$, and the transform in $c$ of this sum at $\pm\zeta$ is that of its $\zeta$ term alone. Here $\gamma'=\gamma^\star\norm\theta_{2,3}^3>0$. Comparing transforms in $c$, frequency by frequency,
\[
  B_\zeta(a,b,c)=\frac{2\gamma'}p\cos\bigl(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c)\bigr)\qquad\text{for every }\zeta .
\]
The right side is not zero, so some neuron carries frequency $\zeta$. Expand $B_\zeta$ as in the proof of \cref{lem:grokking:block}, with the positive weights $\beta_r=\rho_r^3$: the character $e^{\grokii\omega_\zeta(a+b-c)}$ occurs only in the clock term, so comparing its coefficients gives $\frac12\sum_r\beta_r=\frac{2\gamma'}p$, and $B_\zeta$ has exactly the form \eqref{eq:grokking:block}. By \cref{lem:grokking:block} the moment conditions hold; and by \cref{lem:grokking:six} at least six neurons carry $\zeta$.

\emph{Width.} Six neurons for each of the $\frac{p-1}2$ frequencies make $m\ge3(p-1)$ necessary. For $m\ge3(p-1)$, use the six-neuron blocks of \cref{rem:grokking:designs} at every frequency and set the other neurons to zero. Multiplying $u$ and $u'$ by $t$ and $v$ by $t^{-2}$ does not change a neuron's function, so the neuron $(\cos(\omega a+\varphi),\cos(\omega b+\psi),\frac13\cos(\omega c+\chi))$ may be replaced by the Fourier neuron $3^{-1/3}(\cos(\omega a+\varphi),\cos(\omega b+\psi),\cos(\omega c+\chi))$. The logits are \eqref{eq:grokking:target}, so all margins are equal and the first inequality in \eqref{eq:grokking:chain} is an equality; every neuron is a Fourier neuron, so the second is one as well.
\end{proof}

The max-margin networks thus form a large family, one positive cubature rule per frequency, but they all compute the clock sum \eqref{eq:grokking:target} up to scale and share three visible features: single-frequency neurons, additive phases, and all $\frac{p-1}2$ frequencies present. \Textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024} prove parts (i) and (ii) for width $m\ge4(p-1)$ with their eight-neuron blocks; the six-neuron blocks and \cref{lem:grokking:six} make the threshold exact. They also treat sparse parities and finite groups, where irreducible representations replace characters.

\section{The solution: why the circuit comes late}\label{sec:grokking:delay}

The max-margin theorem describes the end of training. Why is the end reached so long after the training set is fit? Two groups gave the same answer in 2023 \parencite{LyuEtAl2024,KumarEtAl2024}: training starts in the lazy regime of \cref{ch:lazy}, where the network fits the training set with its initial tangent kernel and memorizes, and reaches the rich regime only later.

\begin{theorem}[\textcite{LyuEtAl2024}; stated without proof]\label{thm:grokking:lyu}
Let $f(\theta;x)$ be $L$-homogeneous and twice continuously differentiable in $\theta$, let $f(\bar\theta;x)=0$ for every input $x$, and run gradient flow on $\Lhat(\theta)+\frac\lambda2\norm\theta^2$ from $\theta(0)=\alpha\bar\theta$, with $\lambda=\lambda(\alpha)$ of order $\alpha^{-q}$ for a constant $q>0$. Fix a constant $c\in(0,1)$ and let $\alpha\to\infty$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item At time $(1-c)\lambda^{-1}\log\alpha$ the predictor converges pointwise, after normalization for classification, to the kernel predictor of the tangent features $\nabla_\theta f(\bar\theta;x)$: the maximum-$\ell_2$-margin classifier if these features separate the data, or the minimum-norm interpolant for regression with linearly independent features.
\item At suitable times between $\lambda^{-1}\log\alpha$ and $(1+c)\lambda^{-1}\log\alpha$, every limit point of $\theta/\norm\theta$ (classification) or of $\theta$ (regression) is a Karush--Kuhn--Tucker point of $\min\frac12\norm\theta^2$ subject to $y_if(\theta;x_i)\ge1$, respectively $f(\theta;x_i)=y_i$, for all $i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

For diagonal linear networks these are the maximum-$\ell_2$-margin and maximum-$\ell_1$-margin classifiers, and the second needs far fewer samples for a sparse target: that gap is grokking. \Textcite{KumarEtAl2024} observe grokking without weight decay, in a two-layer network learning the square of a linear function of Gaussian inputs by gradient descent, controlled by an output scale that sets the laziness. For modular addition, \textcite{MohamadiEtAl2024} prove that permutation-equivariant kernel methods, a model of the lazy phase, need a constant fraction of the table to generalize.

The diagonal network of \cref{ch:diagonal} with weight decay makes the two phases explicit. Let $X\in\R^{n\times d}$ with $n<d$, let $y=Xw_\star$ with a $k$-sparse target $w_\star$, let $w=u\odot u-v\odot v$ with $u(0)=v(0)=\alpha\mathbf1_d$, and run gradient flow on
\[
  \Lhat(w)+\frac\lambda2\bigl(\norm u^2+\norm v^2\bigr),\qquad \Lhat(w)=\frac1{2n}\norm{Xw-y}^2 .
\]
Recall from \cref{ch:diagonal} the potential $\Phi_\alpha(w)=\frac{\alpha^2}4\sum_iq(w_i/\alpha^2)$ with $q(z)=2-\sqrt{4+z^2}+z\arcsinh(z/2)$, whose gradient is $\nabla\Phi_\alpha(w)_i=\frac14\arcsinh(w_i/2\alpha^2)$. Without weight decay the flow converges to $w^\Phi_\alpha=\argmin\{\Phi_\alpha(w):Xw=y\}$, which is close to the minimum-$\ell_2$-norm interpolator when $\alpha$ is large and to the minimum-$\ell_1$-norm interpolator when $\alpha$ is small.

\begin{proposition}[Weight decay shrinks the initialization]\label[proposition]{prop:grokking:decay}
Let $\alpha_t=\alpha e^{-\lambda t}$ and $g(t)=\nabla\Lhat(w(t))$. For every $t\ge0$ and every coordinate $i$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $u_i(t)v_i(t)=\alpha_t^2$;
\item $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\nabla\Phi_{\alpha_t}(w(t))=-g(t)$;
\item $w(t)=\argmin\{\Phi_{\alpha_t}(w'):Xw'=Xw(t)\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The flow is $\dot u=-2u\odot g-\lambda u$ and $\dot v=2v\odot g-\lambda v$. It exists for all time, because the objective decreases along it and is at least $\frac\lambda2(\norm u^2+\norm v^2)$, so the trajectory stays bounded.

\emph{Part (i).} The product rule gives $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(u_iv_i)=(-2g_i-\lambda)u_iv_i+(2g_i-\lambda)u_iv_i=-2\lambda u_iv_i$, and $u_i(0)v_i(0)=\alpha^2$.

\emph{Part (ii).} Let $\sigma_i=u_i^2+v_i^2$. By part (i), $\sigma_i^2-w_i^2=4u_i^2v_i^2=4\alpha_t^4$, so $\sigma_i=\sqrt{w_i^2+4\alpha_t^4}$, and the flow gives $\dot w_i=2u_i\dot u_i-2v_i\dot v_i=-4\sigma_ig_i-2\lambda w_i$. Let $z_i=w_i/(2\alpha_t^2)$. Since $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\alpha_t^2=-2\lambda\alpha_t^2$, the quotient rule gives $\dot z_i=(\dot w_i+2\lambda w_i)/(2\alpha_t^2)=-2\sigma_ig_i/\alpha_t^2$, while $\sqrt{1+z_i^2}=\sigma_i/(2\alpha_t^2)$. Hence
\[
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\frac14\arcsinh z_i=\frac{\dot z_i}{4\sqrt{1+z_i^2}}=-g_i .
\]
\emph{Part (iii).} At $t=0$, $\nabla\Phi_\alpha(w(0))=0$, so by part (ii) $\nabla\Phi_{\alpha_t}(w(t))=-\int_0^tg=-\frac1nX^\top\int_0^t(Xw-y)$ lies in the row space of $X$. The potential $\Phi_{\alpha_t}$ is strictly convex, because $q''>0$, so a point $w'$ minimizes it on the affine set $\{Xw'=Xw(t)\}$ if and only if $\nabla\Phi_{\alpha_t}(w')$ lies in the row space; $w(t)$ is such a point, and the minimizer is unique.
\end{proof}

On a diagonal network, then, weight decay acts exactly as an initialization scale shrinking like $e^{-\lambda t}$: at every moment the network sits where the flow of \cref{ch:diagonal} from scale $\alpha_t$ would have put it, among predictors with its own training outputs. Nothing here is asymptotic; the rest needs one approximation.

\begin{heuristic}[Heuristic derivation of the grokking time]
Assume that the training residual stays negligible after an initial transient, so that $Xw(t)\approx y$. Then \cref{prop:grokking:decay} gives $w(t)\approx w^\Phi_{\alpha_t}$, and the test error at time $t$ is a fixed function of $\log\alpha-\lambda t$, read off from the static family $a\mapsto w^\Phi_a$.

\emph{Memorization.} At first $\sigma_i\approx2\alpha^2$, so $\dot w\approx-8\alpha^2\nabla\Lhat(w)$, and the residual $Xw-y$ decays at rate at least $8\alpha^2\lmin(XX^\top/n)$. The training set is fit by the time $\grokmem\approx\log(1/\eps)/\bigl(8\alpha^2\lmin(XX^\top/n)\bigr)$, while $w\approx w^\Phi_\alpha$ is close to the minimum-norm interpolator, whose test error for a sparse target is near the null risk.

\emph{Generalization.} The static family passes from $\ell_2$-like to $\ell_1$-like behavior when $a$ crosses a critical scale $\alpha_c$, so the test error drops when $\alpha e^{-\lambda t}=\alpha_c$, that is, at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grokking:tgrok}
  \grokt\approx\frac1\lambda\log\frac\alpha{\alpha_c} .
\end{equation}
\emph{The critical scale.} For $\abs{w_i}\ll a^2$ the potential is quadratic, $\Phi_a(w)\approx\sum_iw_i^2/(16a^2)$; for $\abs{w_i}\gg a^2$ it is $\Phi_a(w)\approx\frac14\sum_i\abs{w_i}(\log(\abs{w_i}/a^2)-1)$. The minimum-norm interpolator $w_2=X^+y$ is spread over all coordinates, with $\norm{w_2}^2\approx\frac nd\norm{w_\star}^2$; the target is concentrated on $k$ of them; and the minimizer switches from one to the other when their potentials are comparable. For a target with entries $\pm1$ this gives, to logarithmic accuracy, $\norm{w_2}^2/(16a^2)\approx\frac14\norm{w_\star}_1\log(1/a^2)$, that is,
\[
  \alpha_c^2\log\frac1{\alpha_c^2}\approx\frac{\norm{w_2}^2}{4\norm{w_\star}_1}\approx\frac{n}{4d}.\qedhere
\]
\end{heuristic}

The ratio $\grokt/\grokmem$ grows like $\alpha^2\log(\alpha/\alpha_c)/\lambda$, so large initialization and small weight decay make grokking dramatic. The same formula, with the weight norm in place of $\alpha$, is the estimate of \textcite{LiuEtAl2023}; here $\alpha_c$ has a definition, and \eqref{eq:grokking:tgrok} refines the $\log\alpha$ of \cref{thm:grokking:lyu} to $\log(\alpha/\alpha_c)$, which matters at moderate $\alpha$. The estimate of $\alpha_c$ is the crudest step: on random instances it is within a factor of about $1.5$ of the scale at which the static test error halves, an error that enters $\grokt$ through a logarithm.

\section{Reading the solution}\label{sec:grokking:reading}

The model makes four quantitative predictions. (i) Every max-margin network has the logits $\gamma(\ind\{c=a+b\}-\frac1p)$, in which the wrong classes tie; as vectors in $\R^p$, the logit vectors of the $p$ possible sums are the vertices of a regular simplex centered at the origin, the geometry of neural collapse (\cref{ch:collapse}). (ii) Each of its neurons has a single frequency, equal norms $\norm u=\norm{u'}=\norm v$, and phase defect $\chi-\varphi-\psi=0$, where $\varphi$, $\psi$, $\chi$ are the phases of $u$, $u'$, $v$ at their common frequency. (iii) All $\frac{p-1}2$ frequencies appear, and the maximal normalized margin is $\gamma^\star=\sqrt{2/27}/(\sqrt p(p-1))$, for instance $6.1\times10^{-4}$ for $p=59$. (iv) In the diagonal caricature the training set is fit at $\grokmem\propto\alpha^{-2}$, the test error drops at $\grokt\approx\lambda^{-1}\log(\alpha/\alpha_c)$, and the test error depends on $\alpha$ and $t$ only through $\log\alpha-\lambda t$.

\Cref{fig:grokking:modadd} tests the features. The network groks, with a delay factor of about $34$, and ends with the neurons that \cref{thm:grokking:neuron} prescribes: the large ones have single frequencies, and their readout phases equal the sums of their input phases to within a few hundredths of a radian, although nothing in training refers to phases. All $29$ frequencies are present, as \cref{thm:grokking:maxmargin} predicts and as \textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024} observed. The Fourier concentration rises long before the test accuracy, a progress measure in the sense of \textcite{NandaEtAl2023}.

The margin predictions fail, informatively. At step $10^4$ the smallest margin is $0.23$ times the average margin, where a max-margin network has all margins equal; the normalized margin is $0.15\,\gamma^\star$; and the clocks carry $59\%$ of the variance of the logits (centered over $c$), against $100\%$ at max margin, so the unwanted terms of \cref{lem:grokking:block} are far from cancelled. Classification needs only the largest logit to be right. The features of the max-margin solution are in place long before its margin is.

The delay roughly follows $\grokt\propto\lambda^{-1}$: with weight decay $0.5$, $1$ and $2$ the test accuracy reaches $99\%$ after about $15000$, $6000$ and $4000$ steps. But with weight decay $4$ the network has not generalized after $40000$ steps (\cref{ex:grokking:wdsweep}): regularization competes with fitting, the confusion phase of \textcite{LiuEtAl2022}, which the diagonal model cannot see.

\section{What the model gets wrong}\label{sec:grokking:wrong}

\paragraph{What was thrown away.} \emph{The task} is an abelian group, whose characters are known in advance and diagonalize everything; natural data have no such basis. \emph{The architecture} is a two-layer network with a polynomial activation, which makes the Fourier analysis exact; there is no attention, no factorization into embedding and unembedding, no normalization, and no ReLU. \emph{The margin theory} describes the end of training on the full table, in the limit of vanishing weight decay and with the $\ell_{2,3}$ norm instead of the $\ell_2$ norm that weight decay penalizes: it explains which features a generalizing network uses, not why training on part of the table generalizes, which is the phenomenon. \emph{The delay model} is a reparameterized linear model whose only ``feature'' is a choice of coordinates; it has no circuit to form.

\paragraph{Where it breaks.} \emph{Different networks find different algorithms.} \Textcite{ZhongEtAl2023} found that transformers with weak attention learn a ``pizza'' algorithm, which averages the embeddings of $a$ and $b$ instead of multiplying them, so that all pairs with the same sum lie on a line through the origin; its logits carry an extra factor $\abs{\cos(\omega(a-b)/2)}$ and are not clocks. Which algorithm appears depends on the architecture, and margin maximization within one architecture does not predict it.\sidenote{Zhong and coauthors vary an attention rate between constant attention, under which the pizza appears, and standard attention, under which the clock appears.} \emph{Transformers use few frequencies.} The transformer of \textcite{NandaEtAl2023} uses five key frequencies, and their other seeds three or four, while every max-margin network of \cref{thm:grokking:maxmargin} at $p=113$ uses all $56$; our MLP agrees with the theory, the transformers do not. \emph{Grokking happens without weight decay.} \Textcite{KumarEtAl2024} observe it with plain gradient descent, controlled by laziness; \textcite{ThilakEtAl2024} with Adam and no regularization, at the onset of cyclic instabilities in which the norm of the last layer jumps; and \textcite{PrietoEtAl2025} show that without regularization the logits grow until floating-point absorption saturates the softmax and the gradient vanishes exactly, halting learning, while the gradient aligns with a direction that scales the logits without changing predictions. \emph{Grokking happens without circuits.} \Textcite{LeviEtAl2024} show that a linear estimator on Gaussian data groks in thresholded accuracy, with a delay computed by random-matrix methods of the kind used in \cref{ch:doubledescent}, while the losses evolve smoothly; and \textcite{VarmaEtAl2023} (unrefereed) explain grokking by the relative efficiency of memorizing and generalizing circuits, which predicts a critical dataset size and ``ungrokking'' below it, an axis that the initialization scale does not capture. \emph{Non-abelian groups.} For finite groups, irreducible representations replace characters, and \textcite{ChughtaiEtAl2023} found networks composing representation matrices, with the representations learned varying from seed to seed; but \textcite{StanderEtAl2024}, reverse-engineering networks trained on $S_5$ and $S_6$, found neurons that detect membership in cosets of subgroups and little evidence that the embeddings store representation matrices. 

\paragraph{Fine structure.} \emph{Representation theory} replaces Fourier analysis for finite groups: \textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024} characterize max-margin networks for general group operations, and \textcite{ChughtaiEtAl2023} describe the algorithm by matrix products. \emph{Algebraic structure}: \textcite{Tian2025} decomposes the squared loss of quadratic networks on abelian groups frequency by frequency and composes global minimizers from small blocks, and reports that about $95\%$ of the solutions found by gradient descent match these constructions. \emph{Phase transitions}: \textcite{RubinEtAl2024} treat grokking in two-layer teacher--student models, with a cubic teacher and with modular addition, as a first-order transition in an adaptive-kernel theory of the Bayesian posterior; \textcite{ZunkovicIlievski2024} compute exact critical exponents in teacher--student models with local rules; and \textcite{BeckEtAl2025} place grokking in logistic regression at the edge of linear separability. \emph{Fourier features elsewhere}: recursive feature machines, kernel machines with learned feature metrics, grok modular arithmetic with block-circulant features that implement the same Fourier algorithm \parencite{MallinarEtAl2025}; GPT-2-XL fine-tuned on addition represents numbers by sparse Fourier features, low frequencies in the MLPs for magnitude and high frequencies in attention for the residue \parencite{ZhouEtAl2024}; and language models place days of the week and months of the year on circles \parencite{EngelsEtAl2025}, a cross-link to \cref{ch:superposition}. Open: a proof that gradient descent with weight decay on a fraction of the table finds the circuit, with the time it takes and the frequencies it selects.

\section{Model card}\label{sec:grokking:card}

\modelcard{A network fits part of a group's table early and generalizes much later; then it computes with characters of the group.}{Two-layer network with quadratic activation and one-hot inputs, on $a+b\bmod p$; for the delay, a diagonal linear network with weight decay and large initialization.}{An explicit Fourier circuit; max-$\ell_{2,3}$-margin networks consist of Fourier neurons with additive phases and use every frequency, with at least six neurons each, which requires width $3(p-1)$; weight decay acts as an initialization scale $\alpha e^{-\lambda t}$.}{Logits $\propto\sum_\zeta\cos(\omega_\zeta(a+b-c))$; $\gamma^\star=\sqrt{2/27}/(\sqrt p(p-1))$; phase defect $0$; $\grokt\approx\lambda^{-1}\log(\alpha/\alpha_c)$, $\grokmem\propto\alpha^{-2}$.}{Transformers use few frequencies or the pizza algorithm; trained networks are far from max margin; grokking without weight decay or without circuits; coset circuits for permutation groups.}{Representation theory of finite groups; algebraic composition of optima; lazy-to-rich dynamics; phase-transition theories; Fourier features in language models.}

Two threads of the book meet here. Implicit bias: the margin argument is that of \cref{ch:margin} for a network homogeneous of degree three, and \cref{ch:diagonal} supplies the potential and the exact effect of weight decay. Lazy versus rich: memorization is the kernel regime of \cref{ch:lazy}, and grokking is the crossing into the rich regime, which weight decay forces at a predictable time; the search phase of \cref{ch:singleindex} and the hidden progress of \cref{ch:parity} tell the same story from small initialization, with the plateau before fitting rather than after. Fourier analysis on groups links the chapter to the parities of \cref{ch:parity} and the circular features of \cref{ch:superposition}, and the equal-margin logits form the simplex of \cref{ch:collapse}.

\begin{exercises}
\item \consolidation (The normalized margin of the circuit.) (a) Show that the network of \cref{thm:grokking:fourier} has normalized margin $0.39\,\gamma^\star$, to two digits. (b) Multiply $u$ and $u'$ by $(2/9)^{1/3}$ and $v$ by $(2/9)^{-2/3}$ in every neuron; show that the logits do not change and the normalized margin becomes $\gamma^\star$. (c) Show that the max-margin logit vectors $f(a,b)\in\R^p$ of the $p$ possible sums form a regular simplex, as in \cref{ch:collapse}. \label{ex:grokking:normalized}

\item \consolidation\hard (Six phases suffice, and tight rules have equal weights.) (a) Verify the six-neuron identity of \cref{rem:grokking:designs}, and check that its points $(\chi_r,\delta_r)$ are $(\frac{\pi r}3,\pi r)$, $r=0,\dots,5$, up to order. (b) Suppose that six points with positive weights satisfy \eqref{eq:grokking:moments}. Show that every weight equals $\frac16\sum_r\beta_r$. (c) Conclude that the nine phases of \cref{thm:grokking:fourier} are not minimal, and that six is the least number of Fourier neurons per frequency. \hint{in (b), the evaluation map from the space of \cref{lem:grokking:six} to the six points is a bijection, so for each point there is a $P_r$ equal to $1$ there and $0$ at the others; compute $\sum_s\beta_s\abs{P_r(\chi_s,\delta_s)}^2$ in two ways and use $1=P_r(\chi_r,\delta_r)\le\sqrt6\,(\sum_{j,s}\abs{c_{js}}^2)^{1/2}$.}\label{ex:grokking:phases}

\item \consolidation (Why the full table.) (a) Redo \cref{lem:grokking:cw} with a non-uniform weighting of the wrong classes, and show that the terms $u(a)^2$ and $u'(b)^2$ no longer cancel in general. (b) Redo it with $(a,b)$ uniform on a training set $D$, and show that the squares cancel if $D$ is a union of rows $\{a\}\times\grokZp$, but not for a typical random $D$. What does this say about explaining generalization from part of the table by margins? \label{ex:grokking:subset}

\item \consolidation (Weight decay and the Lasso.) (a) Show that the minimum of $\norm u^2+\norm v^2$ subject to $u\odot u-v\odot v=w$ is $\norm w_1$; deduce that the regularized objective of \cref{sec:grokking:delay} and the Lasso $\Lhat(w)+\frac\lambda2\norm w_1$ have the same minimum value, and that global minimizers correspond. (b) For gradient descent with step $\eta$, show that $u_iv_i$ is multiplied by $(1-\eta\lambda)^2-4\eta^2g_i^2$ at each step, so part (i) of \cref{prop:grokking:decay} holds up to a relative error of order $\eta^2$ per step. (c) Evaluate $\grokmem$ for the run of \cref{fig:grokking:diagonal} and compare with the figure. \label{ex:grokking:lasso}

\item \variation\hard (Multiplication and even moduli.) (a) Let $g$ generate the cyclic group $\grokZp^\times$ of order $p-1$. Show that for $a,b,c\in\grokZp^\times$, $c=ab$ if and only if $\log_gc=\log_ga+\log_gb$ in $\Z_{p-1}$, and build a Fourier network for multiplication. (b) The modulus $N=p-1$ is even, so the frequency $N/2$, whose character $(-1)^x$ becomes the Legendre symbol, is real and has no conjugate partner. Redo \cref{thm:grokking:neuron} on $\Z_N$ and show that the best single neuron sits at frequency $N/2$, with average margin $2/(3\sqrt3(N-1)\sqrt N)$, a factor $\sqrt2$ above the best paired frequency. (c) Show that if all neurons sit at frequency $N/2$, then every wrong class of the same parity as $a+b$ has margin zero, and conclude that the proof of \cref{thm:grokking:maxmargin} fails for even moduli. \label{ex:grokking:even}

\item \variation (A non-abelian group.) Let $G$ be a finite group with irreducible characters $\chi_\rho$ of dimensions $d_\rho$. (a) Use the orthogonality of characters to show that $\sum_\rho d_\rho\chi_\rho(g)=\abs G\,\ind\{g=e\}$. (b) Write the character table of $S_3$ and check (a). (c) Deduce that the logits $f_c(a,b)=\sum_\rho d_\rho\chi_\rho(c^{-1}ab)-1$ give every margin $\abs G$. (d) Write $\chi_\rho(c^{-1}ab)$ as the trace of a product of three matrices, and explain why a network now needs products of matrix entries; compare with the algorithm of \textcite{ChughtaiEtAl2023}. \label{ex:grokking:s3}

\item \variation (The Euclidean norm.) (a) Show that $\min_{t>0}\bigl(t^2(\norm u^2+\norm{u'}^2)+t^{-4}\norm v^2\bigr)=3\cdot2^{-2/3}(\norm u^2+\norm{u'}^2)^{2/3}\norm v^{2/3}$: the weight-decay cost of a neuron grows like the power $\frac23$ of the size of its contribution to the logits. (b) Show that under $\norm\theta_2\le1$ the average margin is maximized only by networks with a single nonzero neuron, and that such a network does not have equal margins (\cref{thm:grokking:maxmargin}). Conclude that the proof of \cref{thm:grokking:maxmargin} does not apply to the norm that weight decay penalizes. \label{ex:grokking:l2}

\item \variation (Unbalanced initialization.) In the diagonal network start from $u(0)=\alpha\mathbf1_d$ and $v(0)=\alpha'\mathbf1_d$ with $\alpha\ne\alpha'$. Show that $u_iv_i=\alpha\alpha'e^{-2\lambda t}$, that part (ii) of \cref{prop:grokking:decay} holds with $\alpha_t^2=\alpha\alpha'e^{-2\lambda t}$, and that $w(t)$ minimizes $\Phi_{\alpha_t}(w')-\frac14\log(\alpha/\alpha')\sum_iw'_i$ on $\{Xw'=Xw(t)\}$. Which sign of target coordinates does $\alpha>\alpha'$ favor? \label{ex:grokking:unbalanced}

\item \simulation (Weight decay and the delay in the network.) Run the script of \cref{fig:grokking:modadd} with weight decay $0.3$, $0.5$, $1$, $2$ and $4$ for $40000$ steps, and record the first step at which the test accuracy reaches $99\%$. The reader should observe about $15000$, $6000$ and $4000$ steps for $0.5$, $1$ and $2$, consistent with $\grokt\propto\lambda^{-1}$ up to a slowly varying logarithm; more than $30000$ for $0.3$; and no generalization for $4$. Plot the parameter norm against time and compare with the effective scale $\alpha e^{-\lambda t}$ of the diagonal model. \label{ex:grokking:wdsweep}

\item \simulation (The critical scale.) With the script of \cref{fig:grokking:diagonal}: (a) set $\lambda=0$ and check that the test error stays at that of $w^\Phi_\alpha$; (b) for $n/d\in\{0.2,0.3,0.4\}$ and several random designs, compare the scale $\alpha_{1/2}$ at which the static test error halves with the root of $\alpha_c^2\log(1/\alpha_c^2)=\norm{w_2}^2/(4\norm{w_\star}_1)$. The reader should find agreement within a factor of about $1.5$, an error of about $\log1.5\approx0.4$ in $\lambda\grokt$, small against $\log(\alpha/\alpha_c)$ when $\alpha$ is large. \label{ex:grokking:alphac}

\item \research (Dynamics on part of the table.) Prove that gradient descent with small weight decay, from a small random initialization on a random fraction of the table, converges to a network of approximately Fourier neurons, and estimate the time. \Textcite{MohamadiEtAl2024} show that quadratic networks fitting the training set with bounded $\ell_\infty$ norm generalize from far fewer points than kernel methods, and that gradient descent with a small $\ell_\infty$ penalty finds such networks; \textcite{Tian2025} describes global minimizers of a squared loss. The dynamics with weight decay, and the selection of frequencies, are open. \label{ex:grokking:dynamics}

\item \research (The narrowest circuit.) What is the smallest width of a network \eqref{eq:grokking:net} whose logits equal the clock sum \eqref{eq:grokking:target} exactly? Fourier neurons need $3(p-1)$, and four neurons of one frequency give its clock up to a function of $c$ alone (see the discussion after \cref{lem:grokking:six}), which further neurons must cancel. A matching lower bound, and the analogous question for the $\ell_2$ margin problem of \cref{ex:grokking:l2}, are open. \label{ex:grokking:narrow}
\end{exercises}

\notesandrefs
Grokking was reported by \textcite{PowerEtAl2022}, and its Fourier explanation comes from \textcite{NandaEtAl2023}. \Textcite{Gromov2023} trained the quadratic network of this chapter with a squared loss and wrote down an approximate solution with random phases. \Textcite{LiuEtAl2022,LiuEtAl2023} first tied the delay to hyperparameters, and \textcite{LiuEtAl2023} gave the estimate $\lambda^{-1}\log(w_0/w_c)$ that \cref{sec:grokking:delay} derives for the diagonal model. The margin analysis is that of \textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024}, resting on \textcite{WeiEtAl2019} and \textcite{LyuLi2020}; \textcite{Tian2025} composes solutions from small blocks, and the cubature view of \cref{lem:grokking:block,lem:grokking:six} organizes all such designs.

For the delay, \textcite{LyuEtAl2024} prove the dichotomy of early kernel and late margin biases, with diagonal networks and matrix completion as examples, and also exhibit ``misgrokking'', in which the early bias generalizes and the late one does not. \Textcite{KumarEtAl2024} build on the lazy regime of \textcite{ChizatEtAl2019} and the kernel and rich regimes of \textcite{WoodworthEtAl2020}. \Cref{prop:grokking:decay} is a short computation in the setting of \cref{ch:diagonal}. AdamW \parencite{LoshchilovHutter2019} multiplies the parameters by $1-\eta\lambda$ at each step, so $\eta\lambda$ is the decay per step in our experiments.

\paragraph{Translating the literature.} \Textcite{MorwaniEtAl2024} write a neuron as $(u_a+v_b)^2w$: their $u$, $v$, $w$ are our $u$, $u'$, $v$; their frequency $\zeta$ and phases $\theta^*_u$, $\theta^*_v$, $\theta^*_w$ are our $\zeta$, $\varphi$, $\psi$, $\chi$; their class-weighted margin with uniform weighting is our average margin $\grokcw$, their $L_{2,3}$ norm our $\norm\cdot_{2,3}$, and their $\gamma^*$ our $\gamma^\star$; their Fourier transform is unnormalized, like ours. \Textcite{NandaEtAl2023} write $w_k=2\pi k/p$ for our $\omega_\zeta$ with $\zeta=k$. \Textcite{Gromov2023} writes $N$ for the width (our $m$), $n$ and $m$ for the inputs (our $a$ and $b$), $q$ for the output (our $c$), and $\phi^{(1)}$, $\phi^{(2)}$, $\phi^{(3)}$ for our $\varphi$, $\psi$, $\chi$. \Textcite{Tian2025} writes $d$ for the order of the group (our $p$); the order of a block is our number of neurons per frequency. \Textcite{LyuEtAl2024} use our $\alpha$ and $\lambda$, write $\bar\theta_{\mathrm{init}}$ for our $\bar\theta$, and write $T^\pm_c(\alpha)=(1\pm c)\lambda^{-1}\log\alpha$ for the two times of \cref{thm:grokking:lyu}.
