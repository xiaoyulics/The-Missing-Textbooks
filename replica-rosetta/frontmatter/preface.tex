\addchap{Preface}

This book began with a list of textbooks that should exist but do not. One entry observed that many of the exact predictions of high-dimensional statistics and machine-learning theory came from statistical physics: the capacity of the perceptron, the signal strength at which principal component analysis begins to see a planted signal, the error of the best possible estimator in a generalized linear model, the learning curve of ridge regression, the energy at which gradient descent stops in a rugged landscape. Physicists derived them with replicas, cavities, the equations of Thouless, Anderson and Palmer, counts of critical points, and dynamical mean-field theory. Mathematicians have since proved many of them, with interpolation, Gaussian conditioning, comparison inequalities and large deviations, and the proofs rarely look like the derivations. Each literature has excellent books, by Mézard, Parisi and Virasoro, Nishimori, Engel and Van den Broeck, and Mézard and Montanari on one side, and by Talagrand, Bovier and Panchenko on the other. But few readers are fluent in both. A mathematician cannot easily tell which steps of a replica computation carry the argument and which are bookkeeping, and a physicist cannot easily see the physical idea inside a proof. The entry proposed a parallel text: each chapter would take one prediction about inference or learning, derive it as a physicist does, set the derivation beside the rigorous route step by step, prove the central result, and record on a status board what is proved, what is conjectured, and what is known to be false.

The resulting book has a prologue, fifteen chapters in four parts, an epilogue, and an appendix. The prologue derives one number, the capacity $2$ of the perceptron, in four ways. Part~I introduces the two languages on the simplest disordered systems: free entropies and self-averaging, the random energy model, and the Sherrington--Kirkpatrick model, where replica symmetry breaks. Part~II is about Bayesian inference, where the posterior is exactly the Gibbs measure that physics studies and the replica-symmetric formulas become theorems: the Nishimori line, spiked matrices, message passing, and generalized linear models, with the gap between what can be learned and what can be learned efficiently. Part~III is about convex learning problems with Gaussian data, where a single comparison inequality, Gordon's, does the work of the replica computation: the perceptron, ridge regression, the LASSO and robust regression, logistic regression and max-margin classifiers, and kernels. Part~IV asks where algorithms go: the critical points of random landscapes, dynamical mean-field theory, and online stochastic gradient descent. Every chapter derives its prediction as a physicist would, sets the derivation and the proof side by side in a table called the stone, proves its central theorem completely (in general, or in a special case that keeps the idea), measures the prediction in a short script, records its status, and says where the translation fails. The epilogue reads the status boards side by side.

There are two intended readers. The first is a mathematician or statistician who has met the phrase ``the replica method predicts'' in a paper and wants to know what was predicted, why physicists believe it, and what has been proved. The second is a physicist or machine-learning theorist who can run a replica computation and wants to know what a rigorous proof adds and which steps of the computation it justifies. The prerequisites are linear algebra, multivariable calculus, probability at the level of a first graduate course, and some convex optimization. No spin-glass theory and no random-matrix theory beyond the semicircle and Marchenko--Pastur laws are assumed; the appendix proves the Gaussian tools that the book uses and states the rest with sources.

Two cautions belong at the front. First, the status boards record the state of research as of October 2026, and the area moves quickly. Several long-standing predictions were settled in 2026 in preprints that have not yet been refereed, and the authors of some of them write that AI systems did much of the work. The book reports such results as their authors state them, with the arXiv number and version, says so when the authors report AI assistance, and never counts them as proved. Second, a theorem in this book often holds under hypotheses that the physical prediction does not need: Gaussian data, a proportional limit, a known prior, a convex loss, a fixed number of iterations, a fixed time horizon. Each chapter names these hypotheses on its board and again in its last section. A parallel text also simplifies both sides: the physical derivations are given in their cleanest form rather than their historical one, and many proofs are given in a special case that keeps the idea, with the general theorem stated and its source named. The book treats mean-field models only, in which every variable interacts with every other.

\paragraph{A note on authorship.} This book was written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, and conceived and edited by Xiaoyu Li, who proposed the subject, the parallel form, the status boards, and the rule that every example come from inference and learning. Every theorem is stated with its hypotheses and is proved, sketched, or attributed to the source that proves it; some proofs and observations are the book's own and are marked as such. Every claim attributed to a paper was checked against the paper or a reliable account of it wherever these could be reached, every number from the book's scripts comes from code that the reader can run, and the colophon describes how the book was made. Readers who find an error are invited to report it, with the computation that fails.
