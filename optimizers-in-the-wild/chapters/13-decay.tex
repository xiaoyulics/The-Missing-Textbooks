\begin{refsection}[chapters/13-decay.bib]

\begin{chaptersummary}
Pretraining recipes multiply the weights by $1-\eta\lambda$ before every AdamW step, with $\lambda=0.1$, although a weight matrix followed by a normalization layer computes the same function at every scale. When the step has a fixed length in a norm, decoupled decay is exactly the Frank--Wolfe method on the ball of radius $1/\lambda$: we prove that the ball attracts every initialization at rate $(1-\eta\lambda)^t$, that the learning-rate schedule acts as a Frank--Wolfe step-size schedule, and where in the ball consistent and noisy coordinates settle. When the loss is scale invariant, we prove that the norm converges from every initialization to an equilibrium at which the weights rotate by $\arccos(1-\eta\lambda)\approx\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$ per step whatever the size of the gradients, so that only the product $\eta\lambda$ matters, and that the rotation lags behind a changing step size. Momentum, preconditioning, gradients that respond to the effective learning rate, and the effects of decay on generalization are what these idealizations miss.
\end{chaptersummary}

\section{In the wild: a regularizer that does not regularize}\label{sec:decay:wild}

For stochastic gradient descent, weight decay and an $L_2$ penalty\index{L2 penalty} are the same thing: adding $\frac\lambda2\norm{\theta}_2^2$ to the loss adds $\lambda\theta$ to the gradient, and $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta(g+\lambda\theta)$ is $\theta\leftarrow(1-\eta\lambda)\theta-\eta g$. For Adam they differ, because the gradient of the penalty is divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$ with the rest. \Textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019} applied the shrinkage outside the adaptive step and found that this \term{decoupled weight decay}\index{weight decay!decoupled} makes the best decay less dependent on the learning rate and improves Adam's generalization; the result is AdamW\index{AdamW}. Every optimizer of this chapter has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay:update}
  \theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)\,\theta_t-\eta_t\,u_t ,
\end{equation}
where $u_t$ is the optimizer's step direction: the gradient for SGD, $\hat m_t/(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon)$ for AdamW, the orthogonalized momentum for Muon. We say learning rate for $\eta_t$ here and step size in the theory; the two are the same.

\begin{practice}[GPT-3, \textcite{BrownEtAl2020}; LLaMA, \textcite{TouvronEtAl2023}; Moonlight, \textcite{LiuEtAl2025Muon}]
Train with AdamW and decoupled weight decay $\lambda=0.1$. The peak learning rates of the GPT-3 models range from $6\times10^{-5}$ (the largest) to $6\times10^{-4}$ (the smallest), so each step shrinks the weights by a factor between $1-6\times10^{-6}$ and $1-6\times10^{-5}$; the factor follows the schedule, while $\lambda$ stays fixed. Decay the weight matrices; by a convention of open training code (folklore), biases and normalization gains are not decayed. When Muon replaces AdamW on the hidden matrices, keep a decoupled decay of the same form. PyTorch's AdamW multiplies the weights by $1-\eta_t\lambda$, with the default $\lambda=10^{-2}$.
\end{practice}

This does not fit the picture of a penalty.\sidenote{The same argument name means different things: in PyTorch's Adam, \texttt{weight\_decay} adds $\lambda\theta$ to the gradient, an $L_2$ penalty; in AdamW it is decoupled.} AdamW does not minimize the loss plus $\frac\lambda2\norm{\theta}_2^2$, which for billions of weights would dwarf the loss; it keeps them in the ball $\norm{\theta}_\infty\le1/\lambda=10$, up to a factor near one for Adam's memory (\cref{sec:decay:frankwolfe}). And most weight matrices of a transformer feed a normalization layer, directly or through the residual stream, which erases the scale of its input.

\begin{observation}[\textcite{VanLaarhoven2017}; \textcite{ZhangEtAl2019}]\label{obs:decay:elr}
If the output of a weight matrix $W$ passes through batch, layer, or RMS normalization, then the network computes the same function with $W$ and $cW$ for every $c>0$, so an $L_2$ penalty on $W$ does not constrain the function; it changes the scale of $W$, and with it the size of each step relative to $W$, an effective learning rate. \Textcite{ZhangEtAl2019} identified this as one of three mechanisms by which weight decay improves the test error of vision networks.
\end{observation}

\begin{observation}[\textcite{WanEtAl2021}; \textcite{KossonMessmerJaggi2024}]\label{obs:decay:rotation}
With normalization and weight decay, the norm of each scale-invariant weight and its angle of rotation per step settle to a steady state. \Textcite{WanEtAl2021} derived the steady-state angle $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$ for SGD and confirmed their predictions on ImageNet and MS COCO; \textcite{KossonMessmerJaggi2024} called the state \term{rotational equilibrium}\index{rotational equilibrium}, studied it for single neurons under AdamW, Lion, and SGD with momentum, and argued that its balance across layers and neurons is part of why AdamW beats Adam with an $L_2$ penalty.
\end{observation}

\begin{observation}[\textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019}; \textcite{WangAitchison2025}]\label{obs:decay:horizon}
The more batch passes, the smaller the optimal $\lambda$. \Textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019} proposed $\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm{norm}}\sqrt{b/(BT)}$ for batch size $b$, $B$ training points, and $T$ epochs, ``merely one possibility informed by few experiments''. \Textcite{WangAitchison2025} read AdamW's weights as an exponential moving average of its updates over $1/(\eta\lambda)$ iterations, and report that the optimal timescale in epochs is roughly the same across model and dataset sizes.
\end{observation}

\Textcite{LiuEtAl2025Muon} name the addition of weight decay as one of the two changes that made Muon work at scale. A counter-observation: \textcite{LobachevaEtAl2021} found that with batch normalization and weight decay, training can be periodic rather than stationary; the norm shrinks, the effective learning rate grows, training destabilizes without diverging, and a new period begins.\sidenote{\Textcite{DAngeloEtAl2024} argue that for language models trained for about one epoch, weight decay acts on optimization rather than generalization: it lowers the training loss and stabilizes training in low precision. The mechanisms of this chapter are optimization mechanisms.} What, then, does $\lambda$ set in a training run? We give two answers, one per idealization: the radius of a ball the weights cannot leave, and the speed at which scale-invariant weights rotate.

\section{The idealization: normalized steps and scale invariance}\label{sec:decay:idealization}

The first idealization keeps the geometry of the step and nothing else.

\begin{assumption}[Steepest descent with decoupled decay]\label{ass:decay:nsd}
Fix a norm $\norm{\cdot}$ on $\R^d$ with dual norm $\norm{\cdot}_*$, a decay $\lambda>0$, and step sizes $\eta_t>0$ with $\eta_t\lambda\le1$. The iteration is
\[
  \theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)\,\theta_t+\eta_t\lmo(g_t),
\]
that is, \eqref{eq:decay:update} with $u_t=-\lmo(g_t)$, where $g_t$ is whatever vector the optimizer feeds to its linear minimization oracle: a gradient, a stochastic gradient, or a moving average of gradients.
\end{assumption}

AdamW without memory ($\beta_1=\beta_2=0$, $\epsilon=0$) is the $\ell_\infty$ case, $\lmo(g)=-\sign(g)$ (\cref{ch:sign}); Lion with decay is the $\ell_\infty$ case applied to an interpolation of momentum and gradient; Muon with decay is the spectral case, $\lmo(G)=-\polar(G)$, applied to the momentum (\cref{ch:spectral}). Norms, oracles, and dual norms are those of \cref{ch:steepest}. The second idealization keeps only the symmetry that normalization layers create.

\begin{definition}[Scale invariance]\label[definition]{def:decay:scaleinv}
A function $\Loss\colon\R^d\setminus\{0\}\to\R$ is \term{scale invariant}\index{scale invariance} if $\Loss(c\theta)=\Loss(\theta)$ for every $\theta\ne0$ and $c>0$.
\end{definition}

A layer normalization without gain has $\mathrm{LN}(cWx)=\mathrm{LN}(Wx)$ for $c>0$ (up to the constant inside its square root), so a loss that depends on $W$ only through $\mathrm{LN}(Wx)$ is scale invariant in $W$; batch, RMS, and weight normalization and the normalization of queries and keys act alike. Here $\norm{\theta}_2$ is the Euclidean norm of all entries of the weight in question and $\hat\theta=\theta/\norm{\theta}_2$ its direction.

\begin{assumption}[Rotation on a sphere]\label{ass:decay:sphere}
The loss $\Loss$ and each per-example loss $\ell(\cdot;\xi)$ are scale invariant and differentiable; the step direction $u_t$ in \eqref{eq:decay:update} is orthogonal to $\theta_t$; and, in the main theorem, the stochastic gradient has a constant length on the unit sphere, $\norm{g_t}_2\norm{\theta_t}_2=\Gamma$.
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{What was thrown away.} For the first idealization, everything except that each step has a fixed length in a norm; the rates also need a convex loss. For the second, everything except that the loss ignores the layer's scale; the main theorem also freezes the gradient size on the sphere and assumes steps orthogonal to the weights, exact for SGD and false for Adam's preconditioned and momentum steps. Each simplification isolates one mechanism: the ball holds for every sequence of bounded steps, whatever produced them, and the equilibrium needs only orthogonality and a balance of two forces.

\paragraph{What one had to notice.} In \eqref{eq:decay:update} the factor $1-\eta\lambda$ is a weight of a convex combination, $(1-\eta\lambda)\theta+\eta\lambda\cdot(-u/\lambda)$, and for steps of fixed length this is a Frank--Wolfe step. For a scale-invariant layer the gradient step is tangent to the sphere and can only enlarge the norm, while the decay shrinks it; their balance fixes the norm, and the norm fixes how far each step turns the weights.

\section{The theory: decoupled weight decay is Frank--Wolfe}\label{sec:decay:frankwolfe}

To minimize a smooth convex $f$ over a compact convex set $\cK$, the \term{Frank--Wolfe method}\index{Frank--Wolfe method} \parencite{FrankWolfe1956}, or conditional gradient method, minimizes the linearization of $f$ over $\cK$ and moves a fraction $\gamma_t$ of the way to the minimizer:
\[
  v_t\in\argmin_{v\in\cK}\ip{\nabla f(x_t),v},\qquad x_{t+1}=(1-\gamma_t)\,x_t+\gamma_t\,v_t .
\]
It needs only a linear minimization oracle for $\cK$, and its iterates stay in $\cK$.\sidenote{For the $\ell_\infty$ ball a projection is a clipping and as cheap as the oracle. For the spectral ball a projection clips the singular values and needs a singular value decomposition, while the oracle needs only the polar factor, which Muon approximates by Newton--Schulz iterations (\cref{ch:spectral}).} For the ball of a norm the oracle is $\lmo$ rescaled (\cref{fig:decay:pictures}, left). Write $\decayball=\{\theta:\norm{\theta}\le1/\lambda\}$, $\gamma_t=\eta_t\lambda$ and $\Pi_t=\prod_{s<t}(1-\gamma_s)$, with $\Pi_0=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Decoupled weight decay is Frank--Wolfe]\label{thm:decay:frankwolfe}
Let the iteration satisfy \cref{ass:decay:nsd}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item With $v_t=\lmo(g_t)/\lambda$ we have $v_t\in\argmin_{v\in\decayball}\ip{g_t,v}$ and $\theta_{t+1}=(1-\gamma_t)\theta_t+\gamma_tv_t$: every step is a Frank--Wolfe step on $\decayball$ with step size $\gamma_t$ for the linear function $\ip{g_t,\cdot}$.
\item For every $t$, $\theta_t-\Pi_t\theta_0\in(1-\Pi_t)\,\decayball$. Consequently the distance from $\theta_t$ to $\decayball$ satisfies
\[
  \bigl(\norm{\theta_t}-1/\lambda\bigr)_+\le\Pi_t\,\bigl(\norm{\theta_0}-1/\lambda\bigr)_+ ,
\]
with equality for every $t$ when $\lmo(g_s)=\theta_0/\norm{\theta_0}$ for all $s$. So $\decayball$ is invariant and, at a constant step size, attracts every initialization at the rate $(1-\eta\lambda)^t$.
\item Let $\Loss$ be convex and $L$-smooth with respect to $\norm{\cdot}$, let $g_t=\nabla\Loss(\theta_t)$, and let $h_t=\Loss(\theta_t)-\Loss^\star_\lambda$ with $\Loss^\star_\lambda=\min_{\decayball}\Loss$. Then for every $t$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay:fwrec}
  h_{t+1}\le(1-\gamma_t)\,h_t+\tfrac L2\,\gamma_t^2\,\norm{v_t-\theta_t}^2 .
\end{equation}
For $\gamma_t=2/(t+2)$, that is $\eta_t=2/(\lambda(t+2))$, and $D=1/\lambda+\max\{1/\lambda,\norm{\theta_0}\}$, we have $h_t\le2LD^2/(t+2)$ for $t\ge1$, which is $8L/(\lambda^2(t+2))$ if $\theta_0\in\decayball$. For a constant $\gamma_t=\gamma$, $\limsup_th_t\le2L\gamma/\lambda^2=2L\eta/\lambda$, and $h_t\le(1-\gamma)^th_0+2L\gamma/\lambda^2$ for every $t$ if $\theta_0\in\decayball$.
\item If instead $g_t=\nabla\Loss(\theta_t)+\zeta_t$ with an error $\zeta_t$, then \eqref{eq:decay:fwrec} holds with the additional term $2\gamma_t\norm{\zeta_t}_*/\lambda$ on its right side.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The plan is to read (i) off the definition of $\lmo$, to unroll the recursion for (ii), and to repeat the classical Frank--Wolfe argument for (iii) and (iv), keeping track of iterates that start outside the ball.

(i) The map $u\mapsto u/\lambda$ is a bijection from the unit ball onto $\decayball$ and multiplies $\ip{g_t,u}$ by $1/\lambda>0$, so it maps minimizers of $\ip{g_t,\cdot}$ over the unit ball onto those over $\decayball$; and $\eta_t\lmo(g_t)=\eta_t\lambda\cdot\lmo(g_t)/\lambda=\gamma_tv_t$.

(ii) Write $\Pi_{s,t}=\prod_{s\le k<t}(1-\gamma_k)$, so that $\Pi_{0,t}=\Pi_t$ and $\Pi_{t,t}=1$. By induction on $t$, (i) gives
\[
  \theta_t=\Pi_t\theta_0+\sum_{s<t}w_s\,v_s,\qquad w_s=\gamma_s\Pi_{s+1,t} .
\]
The weights are nonnegative because $0<\gamma_k\le1$, and they telescope, $w_s=\Pi_{s+1,t}-\Pi_{s,t}$, to $1-\Pi_t$. If $\Pi_t<1$, then $\sum_sw_sv_s/(1-\Pi_t)$ is a convex combination of points of $\decayball$, so $\sum_sw_sv_s\in(1-\Pi_t)\decayball$; if $\Pi_t=1$, then the sum is zero. The triangle inequality gives $\norm{\theta_t}-1/\lambda\le\Pi_t(\norm{\theta_0}-1/\lambda)$; take positive parts, as $\Pi_t\ge0$. If $\lmo(g_s)=\theta_0/\norm{\theta_0}$ for all $s$, then every $v_s$ is a positive multiple of $\theta_0$ and the triangle inequality is an equality. Finally, in every norm the distance from $\theta$ to $\decayball$ is $(\norm{\theta}-1/\lambda)_+$: if $\norm{\theta}>1/\lambda$, then the point $\theta/(\lambda\norm{\theta})$ of the ball is at distance $\norm{\theta}-1/\lambda$, and every $x\in\decayball$ has $\norm{\theta-x}\ge\norm{\theta}-\norm{x}\ge\norm{\theta}-1/\lambda$.

(iii) The minimum $\Loss^\star_\lambda$ is attained at some $\theta^\star\in\decayball$, since $\decayball$ is compact. The descent lemma (\cref{lem:toolkit:descent}), applied to the step $\gamma_t(v_t-\theta_t)$, gives
\[
  \Loss(\theta_{t+1})\le\Loss(\theta_t)+\gamma_t\ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta_t),v_t-\theta_t}+\tfrac L2\gamma_t^2\norm{v_t-\theta_t}^2 .
\]
Because $v_t$ minimizes $\ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta_t),\cdot}$ over $\decayball\ni\theta^\star$, and $\Loss$ is convex,
\[
  \ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta_t),v_t-\theta_t}\le\ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta_t),\theta^\star-\theta_t}\le\Loss(\theta^\star)-\Loss(\theta_t)=-h_t .
\]
Substituting gives \eqref{eq:decay:fwrec}, which does not use the sign of $h_t$ (negative values occur while $\theta_t$ is outside the ball). By (ii), $\norm{v_t-\theta_t}\le1/\lambda+\norm{\theta_t}\le2/\lambda+\Pi_te_0$ with $e_0=(\norm{\theta_0}-1/\lambda)_+$.

For $\gamma_t=2/(t+2)$ we have $\gamma_0=1$, so the first step lands on the vertex $v_0$ and $\Pi_t=0$ for $t\ge1$; hence $\norm{v_t-\theta_t}\le2/\lambda\le D$ for $t\ge1$, and $\norm{v_0-\theta_0}\le1/\lambda+\norm{\theta_0}\le D$. We show $h_t\le2LD^2/(t+2)$ by induction. At $t=1$, \eqref{eq:decay:fwrec} with $\gamma_0=1$ gives $h_1\le\frac L2D^2\le\frac23LD^2$. If the bound holds at $t\ge1$, then
\[
  h_{t+1}\le\frac t{t+2}\cdot\frac{2LD^2}{t+2}+\frac L2\cdot\frac4{(t+2)^2}\,D^2=\frac{2LD^2(t+1)}{(t+2)^2}\le\frac{2LD^2}{t+3},
\]
because $(t+1)(t+3)\le(t+2)^2$. If $\theta_0\in\decayball$, then $D=2/\lambda$.

For a constant $\gamma$, unrolling \eqref{eq:decay:fwrec} gives
\[
  h_t\le(1-\gamma)^th_0+\frac L2\gamma^2\sum_{s<t}(1-\gamma)^{t-1-s}\Bigl(\frac2\lambda+(1-\gamma)^se_0\Bigr)^2 .
\]
Expanding the square, the sum is at most $4/(\lambda^2\gamma)+(4e_0/\lambda)\,t(1-\gamma)^{t-1}+e_0^2(1-\gamma)^{t-1}/\gamma$, whose last two terms tend to zero, and vanish if $\theta_0\in\decayball$.

(iv) Now $v_t$ minimizes $\ip{g_t,\cdot}$, so $\ip{g_t,v_t}\le\ip{g_t,\theta^\star}$, and with $\nabla\Loss(\theta_t)=g_t-\zeta_t$,
\[
  \ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta_t),v_t-\theta_t}\le\ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta_t),\theta^\star-\theta_t}+\ip{\zeta_t,\theta^\star-v_t} .
\]
The first term is at most $-h_t$ as in (iii); the second is at most $\norm{\zeta_t}_*\norm{\theta^\star-v_t}\le2\norm{\zeta_t}_*/\lambda$ by Hölder's inequality (\cref{lem:toolkit:dualnorm}).
\end{proof}

So the schedule is a Frank--Wolfe step-size schedule, $\gamma_t=\eta_t\lambda$, on a ball of radius $1/\lambda$.\sidenote{The radius depends on what multiplies $\lambda$. In PyTorch the decay per step is $\eta_t\lambda$ and the radius $1/\lambda$; in the AdamW of \textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019} the decay is the schedule multiplier times $\lambda$ while the step also carries the base learning rate $\alpha$, and the radius is $\alpha/\lambda$.} The classical $\gamma_t=2/(t+2)$ is a learning rate that starts at $1/\lambda$ and decays like $1/t$; every schedule with $\gamma_t\to0$ and $\sum_t\gamma_t=\infty$ gives $\limsup_th_t\le0$ (\cref{ex:decay:schedules}), while a constant one leaves a neighborhood of size at most $2L\eta/\lambda$, which a decaying schedule removes (\cref{ch:schedules}). These are worst cases: on the quadratic of \cref{fig:decay:frankwolfe} the gap falls like $1/t^2$, and the constant-step excess is of order $\gamma^2$ (\cref{ex:decay:cycle}). Part (iv) adds a floor that no schedule removes, $2\sigma_*/\lambda$ when $\E\norm{\zeta_t}_*\le\sigma_*$, for the error of a stochastic gradient or of a momentum average (\cref{ch:momentum}), which is one reason to feed a momentum to the oracle, as Lion and Muon do.

For AdamW with memory and full-batch gradients, \textcite{XieLi2024} proved that if the iterates converge under a nonincreasing schedule with divergent sum, then the limit is a Karush--Kuhn--Tucker point of $\min\Loss$ subject to $\norm{\theta}_\infty\le1/\lambda$; \textcite{ChenEtAl2024Lion} showed with a Lyapunov function that Lion with decay solves the same constrained problem, and \textcite{PethickEtAl2025} built optimizers, Muon-like ones among them, directly as conditional-gradient methods. The proof of (ii) uses only that $\norm{\lmo(g_t)}\le1$: steps with $\norm{u_t}\le C$ are drawn into the ball of radius $C/\lambda$ at the same rate. Adam's step is bounded coordinatewise by $(1-\beta_1)/\sqrt{(1-\beta_2)(1-\beta_1^2/\beta_2)}$ when $\beta_1^2<\beta_2$ (\cref{ch:sign}), which is $1.165$ for $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.95)$, so AdamW's weights are drawn into the $\ell_\infty$ ball of radius $1.165/\lambda$.\sidenote{\Cref{ch:sign} proves the bound without bias correction and, in \cref{ex:sign:biascorr}, with it. With bias correction, the Cauchy--Schwarz bound after $t$ steps increases with $t$ to the same limit, as we checked numerically for $(0.9,0.95)$ and $(0.9,0.999)$.} Where in the ball do the weights sit? One coordinate of sign descent answers exactly.

\begin{proposition}[Consistent and noisy coordinates]\label[proposition]{prop:decay:regimes}
Let $0<\gamma=\eta\lambda<1$, $x_0\in\R$, and $x_{t+1}=(1-\gamma)x_t-\eta s_t$, where $\abs{s_t}\le1$ and $\E[s_t\mid\cF_t]=\mu$ for every $t$, with $\cF_t$ generated by $s_0,\dots,s_{t-1}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\E x_t=-\mu/\lambda+(1-\gamma)^t(x_0+\mu/\lambda)$; if $s_t=\mu\in\{-1,1\}$ is deterministic, then $x_t$ follows this curve to the vertex $-\mu/\lambda$.
\item If $\mu=0$ and $\E[s_t^2\mid\cF_t]=1$, as for random signs, then $\E x_t^2=(1-\gamma)^{2t}x_0^2+\eta^2\bigl(1-(1-\gamma)^{2t}\bigr)/\bigl(1-(1-\gamma)^2\bigr)$, which tends to $\eta/(\lambda(2-\eta\lambda))$: the coordinate settles at root mean square $\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\gamma/(2-\gamma)}\approx\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\eta\lambda/2}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Taking expectations, $\E x_{t+1}=(1-\gamma)\E x_t-\eta\mu$, whose solution is (i). For (ii), $\E[x_ts_t]=\E[x_t\,\E[s_t\mid\cF_t]]=0$ because $x_t$ is determined by $\cF_t$, so $\E x_{t+1}^2=(1-\gamma)^2\E x_t^2+\eta^2$.
\end{proof}

Under sign descent, a coordinate whose gradient has mean $\bar g_i$ and Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma_i$ has $\abs\mu=\erf(\abs{\bar g_i}/(\sigma_i\sqrt2))$: it settles at the vertex when the gradient is consistent and deep inside when it is noise, at $0.2\%$ to $0.5\%$ of the radius for the GPT-3 values $\eta\lambda\in[6\times10^{-6},6\times10^{-5}]$. The ball constrains only coordinates whose updates keep one sign for about $1/(\eta\lambda)$ steps; for the others the decay balances noise against shrinkage, and for scale-invariant weights this balance is the whole story.

\section{The theory: scale invariance and the rotational equilibrium}\label{sec:decay:equilibrium}

Gradients in this section are Euclidean. Two facts make scale-invariant weights special.

\begin{lemma}[Scale invariance]\label[lemma]{lem:decay:scaleinv}
Let $\Loss$ be scale invariant and twice differentiable on $\R^d\setminus\{0\}$. For every $\theta\ne0$ and $c>0$:
(i) $\ip{\nabla\Loss(\theta),\theta}=0$;
(ii) $\nabla\Loss(c\theta)=c^{-1}\nabla\Loss(\theta)$, so $\nabla\Loss(\theta)=\nabla\Loss(\hat\theta)/\norm{\theta}_2$;
(iii) $\nabla^2\Loss(c\theta)=c^{-2}\nabla^2\Loss(\theta)$, so the sharpness is $S(\theta)=S(\hat\theta)/\norm{\theta}_2^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate $\Loss(c\theta)=\Loss(\theta)$ in $c$ at $c=1$ for (i), and in $\theta$, once and twice, for $c\nabla\Loss(c\theta)=\nabla\Loss(\theta)$ and $c^2\nabla^2\Loss(c\theta)=\nabla^2\Loss(\theta)$; then take $c=\norm{\theta}_2$ and replace $\theta$ by $\hat\theta$.
\end{proof}

So a gradient step is tangent to the sphere, and gradient and curvature fall as the norm grows; by (iii), the stability threshold $\eta S<2$ of \cref{ch:eos} reads $(\eta/\norm{\theta}_2^2)\,S(\hat\theta)<2$. If each per-example loss is scale invariant, then (i) holds for every stochastic gradient, and a step of \eqref{eq:decay:update} is a right triangle (\cref{fig:decay:pictures}, right).

\begin{lemma}[One step]\label[lemma]{lem:decay:step}
Let $\theta\ne0$, $0<\eta\lambda<1$, $\theta'=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta-\eta u$ with $\ip{u,\theta}=0$, and let $\omega$ be the angle between $\theta$ and $\theta'$. Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\norm{\theta'}_2^2=(1-\eta\lambda)^2\norm{\theta}_2^2+\eta^2\norm{u}_2^2$;
\item $\cos\omega=(1-\eta\lambda)\norm{\theta}_2/\norm{\theta'}_2$ and $\tan\omega=\eta\norm{u}_2/((1-\eta\lambda)\norm{\theta}_2)$;
\item if $u\ne0$, then $\hat\theta'=(\hat\theta-\alpha\hat u)/\norm{\hat\theta-\alpha\hat u}_2$ with $\hat u=u/\norm{u}_2$ and $\alpha=\tan\omega$;
\item $\norm{\theta'-\theta}_2^2/\norm{\theta}_2^2=\eta^2\lambda^2+\eta^2\norm{u}_2^2/\norm{\theta}_2^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Pythagoras' theorem for the orthogonal vectors $\theta$ and $u$, with $\ip{\theta,\theta'}=(1-\eta\lambda)\norm{\theta}_2^2$ for (ii); for (iii), divide $\theta'$ by $(1-\eta\lambda)\norm{\theta}_2>0$; for (iv), $\theta'-\theta=-\eta\lambda\theta-\eta u$.
\end{proof}

Write $a=(1-\eta\lambda)^2$; then $\cos\omega=\sqrt a$ exactly when the norm does not change. The weakest form of the equilibrium needs nothing more; we call $\omega_t$ the \term{angular update}\index{angular update}.

\begin{proposition}[The equilibrium angle is a time average]\label[proposition]{prop:decay:average}
Let $\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_t-\eta u_t$ with $\ip{u_t,\theta_t}=0$, $\theta_t\ne0$, constant $\eta$ and $\lambda$ with $0<\eta\lambda<1$, and $q_t=\norm{\theta_t}_2^2$. For all $T_1<T_2$,
\[
  \sum_{t=T_1}^{T_2-1}q_t\tan^2\omega_t=\frac{1-a}a\sum_{t=T_1}^{T_2-1}q_t+\frac{q_{T_2}-q_{T_1}}a .
\]
If $q_{T_2}=q_{T_1}$, then the $q$-weighted mean of $\tan^2\omega_t$ over the window is exactly $(1-a)/a=\tan^2\bigl(\arccos(1-\eta\lambda)\bigr)$, whatever the sizes of the steps.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:decay:step}(i) and (ii), $aq_t\tan^2\omega_t=\eta^2\norm{u_t}_2^2=q_{t+1}-aq_t=(q_{t+1}-q_t)+(1-a)q_t$. Sum over the window and divide by $a$.
\end{proof}

Gradients may be large in some steps and small in others, but once decay and growth cancel on average, the mean rotation is fixed by $\eta\lambda$ alone; this is why measured angles are robust. With a constant gradient size on the sphere, the norm is driven to that balance from every start.

\begin{theorem}[Rotational equilibrium of SGD]\label{thm:decay:equilibrium}
Let $\theta_0\ne0$, $0<\eta\lambda<1$, and $\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_t-\eta g_t$ with $\ip{g_t,\theta_t}=0$ and $\norm{g_t}_2\norm{\theta_t}_2=\Gamma>0$ for every $t$. Let $q_t=\norm{\theta_t}_2^2$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay:qinf}
  q_\infty=\frac{\eta\Gamma}{\sqrt{1-a}}=\Gamma\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{\lambda(2-\eta\lambda)}}\approx\Gamma\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{2\lambda}} .
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $q_{t+1}=aq_t+\eta^2\Gamma^2/q_t$, and $q_t\to q_\infty$ for every $\theta_0\ne0$; the distance $\abs{\log(q_t/q_\infty)}$ never increases, and decreases strictly unless $q_t=q_\infty$.
\item Suppose $\eta\lambda\le1-1/\sqrt2$, so that $a\ge\frac12$. If $q_t\ge q_\infty$, then $0\le q_{t+1}-q_\infty\le a\,(q_t-q_\infty)$. If $q_t<q_\infty$, then $q_{t+1}>q_t$, and either $q_{t+1}\ge q_\infty$ or $0<q_\infty-q_{t+1}\le(2a-1)(q_\infty-q_t)$. Near $q_\infty$ the error is multiplied by $2a-1=1-4\eta\lambda+2\eta^2\lambda^2$ per step.
\item As $t\to\infty$: the angle $\omega_t$ between $\theta_t$ and $\theta_{t+1}$ tends to $\omega_\infty=\arccos(1-\eta\lambda)=\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}\,(1+O(\eta\lambda))$; the relative step $\norm{\theta_{t+1}-\theta_t}_2/\norm{\theta_t}_2$ tends to $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$ exactly; and the direction moves on the unit sphere by $\hat\theta_{t+1}=(\hat\theta_t-\eta_{\mathrm{eff},t}\hat g_t)/\norm{\hat\theta_t-\eta_{\mathrm{eff},t}\hat g_t}_2$ with $\hat g_t=\norm{\theta_t}_2\,g_t$, which is the gradient at $\hat\theta_t$ when $g_t=\nabla\ell(\theta_t;\xi_t)$ for a scale-invariant $\ell$; the \term{effective step size}\index{effective learning rate} $\eta_{\mathrm{eff},t}=\eta/((1-\eta\lambda)q_t)$ tends to $\tan(\omega_\infty)/\Gamma$.
\item If instead $\Gamma_t=\norm{g_t}_2\norm{\theta_t}_2\in[\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2]$ for every $t$, with $a\ge\frac12$ and $\Gamma_1/\Gamma_2>(1-a)/a$, then $q_\infty(\Gamma_1)\le\liminf_tq_t\le\limsup_tq_t\le q_\infty(\Gamma_2)$, where $q_\infty(\Gamma)$ is given by \eqref{eq:decay:qinf}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The plan is to reduce the dynamics to one scalar map, to show that the map moves every point toward its fixed point on a logarithmic scale, and to read the angles off \cref{lem:decay:step}.

(i) \Cref{lem:decay:step}(i) with $\norm{g_t}_2^2=\Gamma^2/q_t$ gives the recursion, and $q_{t+1}\ge aq_t>0$. Put $x_t=q_t/q_\infty$. Since $\eta^2\Gamma^2=(1-a)q_\infty^2$, dividing by $q_\infty$ gives $x_{t+1}=\varphi(x_t)$ with
\[
  \varphi(x)=a\,x+(1-a)\,\frac1x ,
\]
a convex combination, with both weights in $(0,1)$, of $x$ and $1/x$. If $x\ne1$, then these two numbers lie on opposite sides of $1$ and $\varphi(x)$ lies strictly between them, so $\abs{\log\varphi(x)}<\abs{\log x}$. The numbers $\ell_t=\abs{\log x_t}$ therefore decrease to a limit $\ell\ge0$. Suppose $\ell>0$. The continuous function $y\mapsto\abs y-\abs{\log\varphi(e^y)}$ is positive on the compact set $\{y:\ell\le\abs y\le\ell_0\}$, which contains every $\log x_t$, so it has a positive minimum $\delta$ there, and $\ell_t\le\ell_0-t\delta$, which contradicts $\ell_t\ge\ell$ for $t>\ell_0/\delta$. Hence $\ell=0$.

(ii) Factor the distances:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay:factor}
  \varphi(x)-1=(x-1)\Bigl(a-\frac{1-a}x\Bigr),\qquad \varphi(x)-x=(1-a)\,\frac{1-x^2}x .
\end{equation}
If $x\ge1$, then the second factor of the first identity lies in $[2a-1,a)\subseteq[0,a)$ because $a\ge\frac12$, so $0\le\varphi(x)-1\le a(x-1)$. If $x<1$, then the second identity gives $\varphi(x)>x$, and the second factor of the first is less than $2a-1$: either it is at most zero and $\varphi(x)\ge1$, or it is positive and $0<1-\varphi(x)<(2a-1)(1-x)$. Multiply by $q_\infty$. The local factor is $\varphi'(1)=2a-1$.

(iii) Since $q_{t+1}/q_t\to1$, \cref{lem:decay:step}(ii) gives $\cos\omega_t=(1-\eta\lambda)\sqrt{q_t/q_{t+1}}\to1-\eta\lambda$, and \cref{lem:decay:step}(iv) gives $\norm{\theta_{t+1}-\theta_t}_2^2/q_t=\eta^2\lambda^2+\eta^2\Gamma^2/q_t^2\to\eta^2\lambda^2+(1-a)=2\eta\lambda$; the expansion $\arccos(1-z)=\sqrt{2z}\,(1+z/12+O(z^2))$ gives the size of $\omega_\infty$. For the direction, \cref{lem:decay:step}(iii) gives the update with $\alpha_t\hat u_t=\eta g_t/((1-\eta\lambda)\norm{\theta_t}_2)=\eta_{\mathrm{eff},t}\hat g_t$, and \cref{lem:decay:scaleinv}(ii) for $\ell(\cdot;\xi_t)$ gives $\hat g_t=\nabla\ell(\hat\theta_t;\xi_t)$. The limit of $\eta_{\mathrm{eff},t}$ is $\eta/((1-\eta\lambda)q_\infty)=\sqrt{1-a}/(\sqrt a\,\Gamma)$, and $\sqrt{1-a}/\sqrt a=\tan\omega_\infty$ because $\cos\omega_\infty=\sqrt a$.

(iv) Let $b_t=\eta^2\Gamma_t^2$, $\underline b=\eta^2\Gamma_1^2$, $\bar b=\eta^2\Gamma_2^2$, $\underline q=q_\infty(\Gamma_1)=\sqrt{\underline b/(1-a)}$ and $\bar q=q_\infty(\Gamma_2)=\sqrt{\bar b/(1-a)}$, so that $q_{t+1}=aq_t+b_t/q_t$ and $\underline b\le b_t\le\bar b$. \emph{Lower bound.} If $q_t<\underline q$, then $q_{t+1}-q_t\ge\underline b/q_t-(1-a)q_t=(1-a)(\underline q^2-q_t^2)/q_t\ge(1-a)(\underline q-q_t)$, so $\underline q-q_{t+1}\le a(\underline q-q_t)$; if $q_t\ge\underline q$, then $q_{t+1}\ge aq_t+\underline b/q_t\ge\underline q$ by (ii) with $\Gamma=\Gamma_1$. Hence $(\underline q-q_t)_+\le a^t(\underline q-q_0)_+$. \emph{Upper bound.} If $q_t\ge\bar q$, then $q_{t+1}\le aq_t+\bar b/\bar q=aq_t+(1-a)\bar q$, so $q_{t+1}-\bar q\le a(q_t-\bar q)$. If $q_t<\bar q$, then $q_{t+1}\le aq_t+\bar b/q_t$, which by \eqref{eq:decay:factor} with $x=q_t/\bar q$ is at most $\bar q$ once $q_t\ge(1-a)\bar q/a$. Since $\underline q/\bar q=\Gamma_1/\Gamma_2>(1-a)/a$, the lower bound gives $q_t\ge(1-a)\bar q/a$ for all large $t$, and from then on $(q_{t+1}-\bar q)_+\le a(q_t-\bar q)_+$.
\end{proof}

The time scales of the \term{equilibrium norm}\index{equilibrium norm} differ from a natural guess. Far above equilibrium the squared norm decays like $a^t\approx e^{-2\eta\lambda t}$, as if the gradient were absent; near equilibrium it relaxes at the rate $4\eta\lambda$, twice as fast, because a smaller norm means a larger gradient (\cref{lem:decay:scaleinv}(ii)); from far below it rises like $q_\infty\sqrt{2(1-a)t}$ and reaches $0.8\,q_\infty$ after about $1/(4\eta\lambda)$ steps. Optimizers whose step has a fixed size, such as AdamW, Lion, and Muon, lack this restoring force, and their recursion is linear.\sidenote{At $a=\frac12$, that is $\eta\lambda=1-1/\sqrt2$, the map $\varphi(x)=\frac12(x+1/x)$ is Newton's iteration for the square root of $1$, and the local factor $2a-1$ vanishes: convergence is then quadratic.}

\begin{proposition}[Steps of fixed size: AdamW, Lion, Muon]\label[proposition]{prop:decay:adamw}
Let $\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)\theta_t-\eta_tu_t$ with $0<\eta_t\lambda<1$ and $q_t=\norm{\theta_t}_2^2$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\ip{u_t,\theta_t}=0$ and $\norm{u_t}_2^2=\rho^2d$ with $\rho>0$ for every $t$, then $q_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2q_t+\eta_t^2\rho^2d$ exactly. At a constant step size, $q_t-q_\infty=a^t(q_0-q_\infty)$ with $q_\infty=\eta^2\rho^2d/(1-a)=\rho^2\eta d/(\lambda(2-\eta\lambda))$, and $\cos\omega_t\to1-\eta\lambda$.
\item If instead $\E[\ip{u_t,\theta_t}\mid\cF_t]=0$ and $\E[\norm{u_t}_2^2\mid\cF_t]=\rho^2d$, with $\cF_t$ the past up to $\theta_t$, then the same recursion holds for $\E q_t$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Part (i) is \cref{lem:decay:step}(i) and (ii) and the solution of a linear recursion. For (ii), expand $q_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2q_t-2\eta_t(1-\eta_t\lambda)\ip{u_t,\theta_t}+\eta_t^2\norm{u_t}_2^2$ and take conditional expectations.
\end{proof}

In equilibrium the entries have root mean square about $\rho\sqrt{\eta/(2\lambda)}$, the noisy-coordinate value of \cref{prop:decay:regimes}, and the angle is SGD's. Sign steps have $\rho=1$, and an orthogonalized $d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}$ step scaled by $s$ has $\rho^2d=s^2\min\{d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}\}$. Orthogonality is exact for normalized gradient steps $\rho\sqrt d\,g_t/\norm{g_t}_2$, holds on average for sign steps when the gradient on the sphere is symmetric noise, and fails for preconditioned and momentum steps (\cref{sec:decay:reading}). For momentum we have a derivation, not a proof.

\begin{heuristic}
\emph{Momentum and the angle per step.} Model AdamW's step, when the gradient is noise, by $u_t=m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\xi_t$ with independent $\xi_t$ of mean zero and $\E\norm{\xi_t}_2^2=d$, the role of $g/\sqrt{\hat v}$. Then $\E\norm{u_t}_2^2\to d(1-\beta_1)/(1+\beta_1)$, while a sum of $k\gg1/(1-\beta_1)$ consecutive steps has second moment about $kd$, because the weights $(1-\beta_1)\beta_1^j$ sum to one: momentum smooths the walk without slowing its diffusion. If $1/(\eta\lambda)\gg1/(1-\beta_1)$, then the weights average about $1/(\eta\lambda)$ steps, so their squared norm keeps the value $\eta d/(2\lambda)$ of \cref{prop:decay:adamw} (\cref{ex:decay:momentum}), and the angle per step is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay:momentum}
  \omega\approx\sqrt{\frac{\eta^2\E\norm{u_t}_2^2}{\eta d/(2\lambda)}}=\sqrt{2\eta\lambda\,\frac{1-\beta_1}{1+\beta_1}},
\end{equation}
$0.23$ times $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$ for $\beta_1=0.9$, while over $k$ steps the direction still turns by about $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda k}$. In the balance behind \cref{prop:decay:average}, the rest of the decay is offset by the radial component of the step $-\eta m_t$: averaged past gradients were tangent to past directions and point partly inward at the present one, so the step points partly outward.
\end{heuristic}

When the step size changes, the equilibrium moves and the norm follows with a lag.

\begin{proposition}[The rotation lags the schedule]\label[proposition]{prop:decay:schedule}
In \cref{prop:decay:adamw}(i), let $q_\infty(\eta)=\rho^2\eta d/(\lambda(2-\eta\lambda))$ and $\omega_\infty(\eta)=\arccos(1-\eta\lambda)$. If $\eta_t$ is nonincreasing and $q_0\ge q_\infty(\eta_0)$, then $q_t\ge q_\infty(\eta_t)$ and $\omega_t\le\omega_\infty(\eta_t)$ for every $t$. If $\eta_t$ is nondecreasing and $q_0\le q_\infty(\eta_0)$, then both inequalities reverse.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The function $q_\infty$ increases on $(0,1/\lambda)$, and $q_\infty(\eta_t)$ is the fixed point of the increasing affine map $q\mapsto(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2q+\eta_t^2\rho^2d$. So $q_t\ge q_\infty(\eta_t)$ implies $q_{t+1}\ge q_\infty(\eta_t)\ge q_\infty(\eta_{t+1})$; induct. By \cref{lem:decay:step}(ii), $\tan\omega_t=\eta_t\rho\sqrt d/((1-\eta_t\lambda)\sqrt{q_t})$, at most the same expression with $q_\infty(\eta_t)$ in place of $q_t$, which is $\tan\omega_\infty(\eta_t)$. The nondecreasing case reverses every inequality.
\end{proof}

A decaying schedule therefore cools a scale-invariant layer faster than the quasi-static $\sqrt{2\eta_t\lambda}$: the relaxation time $1/(2\eta_t\lambda)$ grows as $\eta_t$ falls, and near the end of a decay to zero the rotation falls like $\eta_t$ rather than $\sqrt{\eta_t}$ (\cref{ex:decay:lag}); a warmup from a small norm rotates faster than its equilibrium. Since only the direction matters, this can be said with a learning rate on the sphere, which is what Hyperball\index{Hyperball} sets.

\begin{proposition}[Weight decay is a Hyperball schedule]\label[proposition]{prop:decay:hyperball}
Let $\Loss$ and each $\ell(\cdot;\xi)$ be scale invariant, and let the base step be $u_t=U(\hat\theta_t,\xi_t)$, a function of the direction and of the randomness $\xi_t$ only, with $\ip{u_t,\theta_t}=0$ and $\norm{u_t}_2^2=\rho^2d$, $\rho>0$. Let $q_t$ solve the recursion of \cref{prop:decay:adamw}(i) from $q_0=\norm{\theta_0}_2^2$, and run Hyperball on the unit sphere, $\hat w_{t+1}=(\hat w_t-\alpha_t\hat U_t)/\norm{\hat w_t-\alpha_t\hat U_t}_2$ with $\hat U_t=U(\hat w_t,\xi_t)/\norm{U(\hat w_t,\xi_t)}_2$, from $\hat w_0=\hat\theta_0$, with the same $\xi_t$ and
\[
  \alpha_t=\frac{\eta_t\rho\sqrt d}{(1-\eta_t\lambda)\sqrt{q_t}} .
\]
Then $\hat w_t=\hat\theta_t$ for every $t$, and the two runs have the same losses. At a constant step size, $\alpha_t\to\tan\omega_\infty\approx\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:decay:adamw}(i), $\norm{\theta_t}_2^2=q_t$. If $\hat w_t=\hat\theta_t$, then $\hat U_t=\hat u_t$, and \cref{lem:decay:step}(iii) gives $\hat\theta_{t+1}=\hat w_{t+1}$. Induct; scale invariance gives equal losses; the limit follows from $q_t\to q_\infty$.
\end{proof}

The hypotheses hold exactly for normalized gradient steps,\sidenote{So a Hyperball learning rate $\alpha$ is an angle per step: $\alpha=0.01$ matches the equilibrium of weight decay with $\eta\lambda\approx\alpha^2/2=5\times10^{-5}$.} whose direction depends on $\theta$ only through $\hat\theta$ by \cref{lem:decay:scaleinv}(ii), and approximately for sign, orthogonalized, and momentum steps, up to a radial component that averages out and a norm that changes slowly. The last result looks at the weights themselves.

\begin{proposition}[AdamW is an exponential moving average of its updates]\label[proposition]{prop:decay:ema}
For \eqref{eq:decay:update} with $0<\eta_t\lambda\le1$ and $\Pi_{s,t}=\prod_{s\le k<t}(1-\eta_k\lambda)$,
\[
  \theta_t=\Pi_{0,t}\,\theta_0-\sum_{s<t}\eta_s\,\Pi_{s+1,t}\,u_s ,\qquad \Pi_{s,t}\le\exp\Bigl(-\lambda\sum_{s\le k<t}\eta_k\Bigr).
\]
At a constant step size, $\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)^t\theta_0+\sum_{s<t}\eta\lambda(1-\eta\lambda)^{t-1-s}(-u_s/\lambda)$, an exponential moving average of the points $-u_s/\lambda$ with timescale $\tau=1/(\eta\lambda)$ steps, in which updates older than $k$ steps carry total weight at most $(1-\eta\lambda)^k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $t$; $1-x\le e^{-x}$; and $\sum_{s<t-k}\eta\lambda(1-\eta\lambda)^{t-1-s}\le(1-\eta\lambda)^k$.
\end{proof}

This is \cref{thm:decay:frankwolfe}(ii) read as an average, and $\tau$ is the \term{timescale of weight decay}\index{weight decay!timescale}. A run covers $\lambda\sum_t\eta_t$ timescales,\sidenote{This average is over updates, with weights fixed by the decay; the model averages of \cref{ch:momentum} average the iterates, with weights chosen freely.} $\eta\lambda T$ at a constant step size, and if the right timescale $\tau$ is a fixed fraction of the run, as the evidence of \textcite{WangAitchison2025} suggests (\cref{obs:decay:horizon}), then $\eta\lambda T$ should be held fixed: at fixed learning rate and batch size, $\lambda$ should fall like the inverse of the number of training tokens, not its inverse square root as in the rule of \textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019}. When $\mu$P scales AdamW's hidden-layer learning rate like $1/d_{\mathrm{in}}$ (\cref{ch:modular}), holding $\eta\lambda$ fixed requires $\lambda\propto d_{\mathrm{in}}$; with a fixed $\lambda$, wider models rotate more slowly and reach equilibrium later. Both rules follow from the identity and the hypothesis; neither is proved optimal.

\section{Reading the theory}\label{sec:decay:reading}

\paragraph{Only the product.} In equilibrium the rotation of a scale-invariant weight depends on $\eta$ and $\lambda$ only through $\eta\lambda$ (\cref{thm:decay:equilibrium,prop:decay:adamw}); the quotient $\eta/\lambda$ sets the norm, which the function ignores. So $(\eta,\lambda)\mapsto(c\eta,\lambda/c)$ leaves the equilibrium dynamics of every scale-invariant layer unchanged (\cref{fig:decay:equilibrium}, middle), while embeddings, output layers, gains, and biases see $\eta$ and $\lambda$ separately.

\paragraph{Sizes and times.} At $\eta=3\times10^{-4}$ and $\lambda=0.1$ the equilibrium angle is $7.75\times10^{-3}$ radians per step, $1.8\times10^{-3}$ with $\beta_1=0.9$ by \eqref{eq:decay:momentum}, while over $1000$ steps the direction turns by about $0.24$ radians either way; the norm relaxes by a factor $e$ every $1/(2\eta\lambda)\approx1.7\times10^4$ steps. The number of timescales $\lambda\sum_t\eta_t$ varies widely: with the hyperparameters of \textcite{BrownEtAl2020} ($300$B tokens, cosine decay to $10\%$), the smallest GPT-3 model ($6\times10^{-4}$, batches of $0.5$M tokens) covers about $18$ and the largest ($6\times10^{-5}$, $3.2$M tokens) about $0.3$; if its layers behaved like the idealization, the largest never reached equilibrium.

\section{What the theory misses}\label{sec:decay:misses}

\paragraph{What was idealized away.} \emph{Exact scale invariance:} embeddings, output layers, gains, and biases are not scale invariant, and the matrices that write into the residual stream are so only jointly, not one at a time; for these, $\eta$ and $\lambda$ act separately and the decay does regularize. \emph{A fixed gradient size on the sphere:} in a network $\Gamma_t$ falls with the loss and depends on the effective step itself, since a larger step keeps the direction farther from the minimizers on the sphere; in a teacher--student version of \cref{fig:decay:equilibrium} (left), with a unit teacher and label noise of standard deviation $1$, the student's wandering raised $\Gamma^2$ by about $26\%$ above its value at the teacher, and the squared norm settled about $12\%$ above the prediction made with that value, at the one made with the measured $\Gamma$. \Cref{thm:decay:equilibrium}(iv) brackets such effects but does not model the feedback. \emph{Orthogonal steps:} preconditioners and momentum add to \cref{prop:decay:average} the radial term $-2\eta(1-\eta\lambda)\sum_t\ip{u_t,\theta_t}$, which in our network carried a fifth of the decay budget without momentum and most of it with momentum. \emph{Convexity:} the rates of \cref{thm:decay:frankwolfe} need a convex loss with a useful smoothness constant; the ball statements do not.

\paragraph{Where it breaks.} (1) The equilibrium need not be stable. \Textcite{LobachevaEtAl2021} found periodic cycles with batch normalization and weight decay instead of a steady state. By \cref{lem:decay:scaleinv}(iii) a shrinking norm raises the curvature the step meets, so the instability of \cref{ch:eos} can return in this form, which our theorem, with $\Gamma$ fixed, cannot see. (2) In vision networks weight decay does more than set a rotation speed \parencite{ZhangEtAl2019}, and nothing here predicts test error. (3) Pretraining does not end at the constrained solutions of the Frank--Wolfe view: at $\lambda=0.1$ the ball is far larger than the weights, and the limit theorem of \textcite{XieLi2024} assumes that the iterates converge; the constraint matters for consistent coordinates and for the implicit bias of long training (\cref{ch:bias}). (4) At scale the effective-learning-rate view is supported but not settled, by four unrefereed 2026 preprints. Loss curves of language models with different learning rates and norms collapse, below the variation between seeds, when indexed by the effective learning rate, and weight decay and Hyperball act mainly through the effective-learning-rate schedules they induce \parencite{LiuEtAl2026ELR}. Fixing the norms with Hyperball speeds up Muon by $20$ to $30\%$ in token-equivalent terms over weight-decay baselines up to $1.2$B parameters and improves the transfer of the learning rate across widths and depths \parencite{WenEtAl2026Hyperball}, which suggests that the equilibrium is not the best way to set the effective learning rate; the difference has been attributed to the time course of the effective step size rather than to a better direction \parencite{XiaoEtAl2026}, and for scale-invariant networks the two methods are dynamically equivalent \parencite{YuanEtAl2026}, as in \cref{prop:decay:hyperball}. (5) For the largest runs the transient, not the equilibrium, may be the regime of interest (\cref{sec:decay:reading}).

\paragraph{Repairs and open problems.} Fixing the norm removes the transient and makes the angle a hyperparameter: Hyperball, and the rotational variants of AdamW, SGD, and Lion of \textcite{KossonMessmerJaggi2024}, which control the rotation explicitly and reduce the need for warmup (\cref{ch:warmup}). \Cref{prop:decay:hyperball} says what such a method must do to imitate weight decay, and \cref{prop:decay:schedule} what it gains: an angle that follows the schedule without lag. Scaling rules for $\lambda$ \parencite{LoshchilovHutter2019,WangAitchison2025} rest on hypotheses about the right number of timescales that no theory derives. Open: a model in which $\Gamma$ depends on the effective step and the stability of the equilibrium, or the period of the cycles, can be computed (\cref{ex:decay:feedback}); a theory of decay for parameters that are not scale invariant; and decay and schedule together in the river valley of \cref{ch:schedules} (\cref{ex:decay:river}).

\section{Back in the wild}\label{sec:decay:back}

\fieldnotes{AdamW with decoupled decay $\lambda=0.1$ at $\eta\sim10^{-4}$; decay for Muon; rescaling $\lambda$ with training length and width.}
{(a) Steps of fixed length in a norm with decoupled decay. (b) A scale-invariant loss, steps orthogonal to the weights, constant gradient size on the sphere.}
{Frank--Wolfe on the ball of radius $1/\lambda$, attracting at rate $(1-\eta\lambda)^t$; global convergence of the norm; angle $\arccos(1-\eta\lambda)$, exact on average at a stationary norm; lag under schedules; Hyperball equivalence; AdamW is an EMA of its updates.}
{Only $\eta\lambda$ matters in equilibrium: $7.7\times10^{-3}$ rad per step at $\eta\lambda=3\times10^{-5}$, after about $1/(2\eta\lambda)$ steps; momentum shrinks the angle per step by $\sqrt{(1-\beta_1)/(1+\beta_1)}$ (derived).}
{Layers that are not scale invariant; gradients that respond to the effective step; radial components of Adam and momentum steps; generalization; periodic instabilities.}
{Treat $\eta\lambda$ as the hyperparameter of scale-invariant layers (proved in the idealization); keep $\eta\lambda T$ fixed as runs lengthen (suggested); expect the effective learning rate to lag the schedule (proved in the model).}

The theory tells a practitioner what $\lambda$ does, not what it should be. For layers that feed a normalization the decay is a speed: once the norm has settled, the weights turn by about $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$ radians per step whatever the gradients, and a change of learning rate that keeps $\eta\lambda$ fixed changes nothing after the transient. The ball of radius $1/\lambda$ is real but, at $\lambda=0.1$, far from the weights of a language model; it shapes where long training ends (\cref{ch:bias}) more than how pretraining proceeds. Which product is best, how many timescales a run should cover, and whether an equilibrium beats a fixed norm are empirical questions, and the 2026 evidence on them is unrefereed.

The Frank--Wolfe identity is the normalized steepest descent of \cref{ch:steepest}, in the norms of \cref{ch:sign,ch:spectral}, made convergent by its schedule, the thread of \cref{ch:momentum,ch:schedules}; the equilibrium angle is the per-layer learning rate of $\mu$P (\cref{ch:modular}) and the angular update of warmup (\cref{ch:warmup}) seen from the sphere; and by \cref{lem:decay:scaleinv}(iii) the stability threshold of \cref{ch:eos} for a scale-invariant layer is a threshold on $\eta/\norm{\theta}_2^2$, which the decay controls.

\begin{exercises}
\item \consolidation (Frank--Wolfe on an interval.) Let $f(\theta)=\frac h2\theta^2$ and $\theta_{t+1}=(1-\gamma_t)\theta_t-\gamma_t\lambda^{-1}\sign(\theta_t)$. (a) For a constant $\gamma\in(0,1)$, show that the two-cycle $\pm\gamma/(\lambda(2-\gamma))$ attracts every start whose orbit avoids $0$ (a fixed point, as $\sign0=0$), so that $f$ settles at $\frac h2\gamma^2/(\lambda^2(2-\gamma)^2)$, about $\gamma/16$ times the guarantee $2h\gamma/\lambda^2$. (b) For $\gamma_t=2/(t+2)$ and $\theta_0\ne0$, show that $\abs{\theta_t}=1/(\lambda(2\lfloor t/2\rfloor+1))$ for $t\ge1$.\label{ex:decay:cycle}

\item \consolidation (The Frank--Wolfe gap.) For convex differentiable $f$ and $\theta\in\decayball$, show that $\mathcal G(\theta)=\ip{\nabla f(\theta),\theta}+\norm{\nabla f(\theta)}_*/\lambda$ is at least $f(\theta)-\min_{\decayball}f$ and vanishes exactly at the minimizers over $\decayball$. For the $\ell_\infty$ ball, deduce that at a minimizer every coordinate has zero partial derivative or sits at $-\sign(\partial_if)/\lambda$.\label{ex:decay:gap}

\item \consolidation (Schedules for Frank--Wolfe.) Let $h_{t+1}\le(1-\gamma_t)h_t+C\gamma_t^2$ with $\gamma_t\in[0,1]$, $\gamma_t\to0$ and $\sum_t\gamma_t=\infty$. Show that $\limsup_th_t\le0$. Compute the bound on $h_T$ for the linear decay $\gamma_t=\gamma_0(1-t/T)$ and compare it with the constant step $\gamma_0$.\label{ex:decay:schedules}

\item \variation (Adam with an $L_2$ penalty.) In Adam with $\beta_1=0$, replace decoupled decay by a penalty, $u_t=(g_t+\lambda\theta_t)/\sqrt{\hat v_t}$ and $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta u_t$, on a scale-invariant layer whose gradient on the unit sphere is noise of variance $\sigma^2$ per coordinate. Assuming $\hat v_t\approx\sigma^2/\norm{\theta_t}_2^2$, show that the decay per step is $\eta\lambda\norm{\theta_t}_2/\sigma$ and that the norm settles at $\norm{\theta}_2^3\approx\eta\sigma d/(2\lambda)$, so that the equilibrium angle depends on the size of the gradient, unlike AdamW's \parencite{KossonMessmerJaggi2024}. (A simulation with $\beta_2=0.999$ agrees to $0.3\%$.)\label{ex:decay:l2}

\item \variation (Momentum, exactly.) Let $\theta_{t+1}=c\theta_t-\eta m_t$ and $m_t=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)\xi_t$ with $c=1-\eta\lambda$ and independent $\xi_t$ of mean zero and covariance $I_d$. (a) Using \cref{lem:toolkit:lyapunov} for $(\theta_{t,i},m_{t-1,i})$, show that the stationary $\E\norm{\theta}_2^2$ is $\eta^2d\,(1-\beta)(1+c\beta)/((1+\beta)(1-c\beta)(1-c^2))=\frac{\eta^2d}{1-c^2}\bigl(1+O(\frac{\eta\lambda}{1-\beta})\bigr)$. (b) Show that $\E\norm{m}_2^2=d(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)$; deduce \eqref{eq:decay:momentum}. (c) For PyTorch's SGD with momentum, $m_t=\beta m_{t-1}+g_t+\lambda\theta_t$ and $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta m_t$, show in the same model that the angle per step is about $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda/(1+\beta)}$.\label{ex:decay:momentum}

\item \variation (MuonW.) A scale-invariant $d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}$ matrix is trained by $W_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)W_t-\eta s\polar(G_t)$ with full-rank gradients $G_t$. (a) Show with a $2\times2$ example that $\ip{\polar(G_t),W_t}$ need not vanish although $\ip{G_t,W_t}=0$. (b) Assuming it averages to zero, compute the equilibrium $\norm{W}_F$; for $s=0.2\sqrt{\max\{d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}\}}$, $\eta=10^{-3}$, $\lambda=0.1$ and $d_{\mathrm{in}}=d_{\mathrm{out}}=1024$, compare the spectral norm of a Gaussian matrix of that Frobenius norm with the radius $s/\lambda$.\label{ex:decay:muon}

\item \variation (The lag under linear decay.) In \cref{prop:decay:adamw}(i), let $\eta_t=\eta_0(1-t/T)$ with $\eta_0\lambda T\gg1$, started at $q_\infty(\eta_0)$. Treating $t$ as continuous, show that the norm tracks $q_\infty(\eta_t)$ while $\eta_t\lambda(T-t)\gg1$ and then freezes, with a remaining fraction of the run of about $(\eta_0\lambda T)^{-1/2}$, after which the angle falls like $\eta_t$.\label{ex:decay:lag}

\item \simulation (Universality.) Run \texttt{13-decay-sweep.py} with $\beta_1=0.5$, with $\beta_2=0.999$, and with width $64$. Check that SGD's angle stays at $\arccos(1-\eta\lambda)$ and that AdamW's with $\beta_1=0.5$ is near $1/\sqrt3$ of it on the first layer, as \eqref{eq:decay:momentum} predicts; measure the radial component of AdamW's step on each layer.\label{ex:decay:sim1}

\item \simulation (Where in the ball.) In \texttt{13-decay-frankwolfe.py}, vary the signal-to-noise ratio of the middle group over $\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}$; check that sign descent settles at $\erf(\mathrm{snr}/\sqrt2)/\lambda$, and measure where AdamW settles.\label{ex:decay:sim2}

\item \research (Feedback and stability.) Let the gradient size on the sphere depend on the effective step, $\Gamma^2=\Gamma_0^2+\kappa\,\decayeff$, as for a student that wanders around a teacher. Find the equilibrium of \cref{thm:decay:equilibrium} and its stability. Is there a natural model, for instance one in which $\decayeff S(\hat\theta)$ is capped at $2$ as in \cref{ch:eos}, where the equilibrium loses stability and the cycles of \textcite{LobachevaEtAl2021} appear? As far as we know this is open.\label{ex:decay:feedback}

\item \research (Weight decay in the river valley.) Combine the river-valley model of \cref{ch:schedules} with a scale-invariant layer whose direction carries the river and the hills. For a given schedule, which $\lambda$ minimizes the final loss, and how does the answer depend on the length of training? Compare with the timescale rule of \cref{prop:decay:ema}.\label{ex:decay:river}
\end{exercises}

\notesandrefs
Decoupled weight decay is due to \textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019}, and the effective-learning-rate view of decay to \textcite{VanLaarhoven2017}. \Textcite{WanEtAl2021} derived the equilibrium angle for SGD with momentum and proved convergence to it at a linear rate under their assumptions; \textcite{KossonMessmerJaggi2024} studied it per neuron for several optimizers; \textcite{LobachevaEtAl2021} found the periodic alternative. \Cref{thm:decay:equilibrium} is the deterministic core of these analyses; we know no earlier statement of \cref{prop:decay:average} in this form.

The Frank--Wolfe method was introduced by \textcite{FrankWolfe1956} for quadratic programs; \textcite{Hazan2016} treats it among projection-free methods. The identification of decoupled decay with Frank--Wolfe on a norm ball runs through \textcite{XieLi2024} for AdamW, \textcite{ChenEtAl2024Lion} for Lion, and \textcite{PethickEtAl2025} for general oracles. The moving-average reading of AdamW and its scaling rules are those of \textcite{WangAitchison2025}. The 2026 literature on fixed norms and effective learning rates is unrefereed \parencite{WenEtAl2026Hyperball,LiuEtAl2026ELR,XiaoEtAl2026,YuanEtAl2026}. The network of \cref{fig:decay:equilibrium} is small; its agreement with the theory checks the identities, not the behavior of large models.

\paragraph{Translating the literature.} \Textcite{LoshchilovHutter2019} write AdamW as $\theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta_t\bigl(\alpha\hat m_t/(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon)+\lambda\theta_{t-1}\bigr)$, with $\alpha$ the learning rate and $\eta_t$ a schedule multiplier: their $\alpha\eta_t$ is our $\eta_t$ and their $\lambda$ is our $\alpha\lambda$. PyTorch's AdamW decays by \texttt{lr}$\cdot$\texttt{weight\_decay} per step and is our $(\eta,\lambda)$ \parencite{Schaipp2024}. \Textcite{WangAitchison2025} write $\tau_{\mathrm{iter}}=1/(\eta\lambda)$ for our $\tau$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{epoch}}=\tau_{\mathrm{iter}}/M$ for $M$ steps per epoch. \Textcite{KossonMessmerJaggi2024} follow one neuron's weight vector, a row of our $W$; their angular update, like that of \textcite{WanEtAl2021}, is our $\omega_t$. Hyperball's step of norm $\eta_tR$ on the sphere of radius $R$ makes its $\eta_t$ our $\alpha_t$ of \cref{prop:decay:hyperball}. \Textcite{LiuEtAl2026ELR} define the effective learning rate as the learning rate divided by the parameter norm, proportional to our $\tan\omega_t$ for steps of fixed size; for SGD our effective step size divides by the squared norm. In the Frank--Wolfe literature $\gamma_t$ is the step size and $\cK$ the constraint set, here $\eta_t\lambda$ and $\decayball$.
