The prologue listed the decisions that a practitioner makes before a training run and promised a theory of each. This epilogue keeps the account. It records, practice by practice, what the chapters proved and in which idealization, what they predicted, and what they left unexplained; it says what the idealizations miss when they are taken together; and it lists the questions that the book raises and cannot answer.

\addsec{A ledger}

\Cref{tab:epilogue:ledger} has one row for each practice. The verdict is one of three. A practice is \emph{explained} when a theorem in a faithful idealization accounts for it and the prediction survives a comparison with simulation or with the evidence from training runs. It is \emph{partly explained} when a mechanism has been identified and proved in an idealization, but either the idealization is far from training or the prediction is only qualitative. It is \emph{unexplained} when the book has nothing to say beyond a description, or when the practice is folklore. The last column says what the verdict rests on, which is where a reader who doubts it should look.

Three things stand out. First, the update rules themselves are well explained: the first line of the book, that optimizers are steepest descent in norms with preconditioning and moving averages, accounts for the form of every rule in the field guide, and it predicts how learning rates must scale with width. Second, almost every practice of the second line is partly explained, and in the same way: a mechanism is proved in an idealization, and the idealization contains one or two parameters, a curvature bound, a noise level, a rate of sharpening, that are measured rather than derived. Third, the numbers that practitioners actually set, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, weight decay $0.1$, a warmup of two thousand steps, a cooldown of a fifth of training, are almost all unexplained. The theory says which combinations matter and how they should scale; it does not say why these values.

\addsec{What the idealizations miss together}

Each chapter listed what its own idealization misses. Read together, the lists repeat five omissions.

\paragraph{The landscape is fixed.} Almost every idealization in the book is an optimizer running on a fixed objective: a quadratic, a convex function, a cubic model of the edge of stability, a valley with a river. The objective of a network changes as it trains. Sharpness rises, features form, norms grow and settle, and the gradient noise changes with them. Where the book needed such a change, it put it in by hand, as the rate of progressive sharpening in \cref{ch:eos} or the loss-dependent curvature in \cref{ch:warmup}.

\paragraph{One mechanism at a time.} Warmup is explained by curvature in \cref{ch:warmup}, by the statistics of Adam's first steps in \cref{ch:sign,ch:adaptive}, and by the size of early angular updates in \cref{ch:decay}; the advantage of Adam on transformers by three mechanisms in \cref{ch:transformers}. In a training run they all operate at once, and nothing in the book says how they combine.

\paragraph{Guarantees, not performance.} Several comparisons in the book are comparisons of worst-case guarantees: which norm gives the better descent bound (\cref{ch:steepest,ch:sign,ch:spectral}), which schedule gives the better last-iterate bound (\cref{ch:schedules}). The chapters checked how far the guarantees are from what the optimizers actually do, and the answer was often a factor of two or more. A guarantee can rank two methods correctly for the wrong reason.

\paragraph{Simple noise.} The noise in most chapters has bounded variance, often Gaussian, often proportional to the curvature. Heavy tails appear only in \cref{ch:spikes}, and the structure of data, such as the Zipf law of token frequencies, only in \cref{ch:transformers}. Both turned out to matter where they appeared.

\paragraph{No scale.} The most consistent finding of careful benchmarks is that the speedups of newer optimizers over a well-tuned AdamW shrink as models grow \parencite{WenEtAl2026Fantastic}. No idealization in this book has a size parameter that reproduces this; the noisy quadratic model of \cref{ch:noise,ch:transformers} comes closest, through the dependence of the critical batch size on the spectrum.

\addsec{What is open}

The chapters end with research exercises. Here are the questions that recur across them, in roughly the order of the book.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The right norm.} The norm of a network is assembled from norms on its layers, but the norms for embeddings, for attention, for normalization gains and for the output layer are chosen by analogy (\cref{ch:modular}). Is there a principle, perhaps from the statistics of activations and gradients, that selects them?
\item \emph{The averaging constants.} Why do $\beta_1=0.9$ and $\beta_2\in[0.95,0.999]$ work so widely (\cref{ch:momentum,ch:adaptive})? The theory explains which timescales these constants set, not why those timescales are right.
\item \emph{Progressive sharpening.} Gradient descent raises the sharpness until the step is barely stable (\cref{ch:eos}). Why does the sharpness rise in the first place? A model in which progressive sharpening is an output rather than an input would change the second half of the book.
\item \emph{Long warmups.} Every explanation of warmup in \cref{ch:warmup} predicts a warmup of tens or hundreds of steps for the settings it treats, while recipes use thousands. What sets the length?
\item \emph{Cooldowns at scale.} The river-valley model predicts the noise floor and the shape of the loss during a cooldown (\cref{ch:schedules}); it does not reproduce the robust parity of warmup--stable--decay with cosine schedules, or the dependence of the best cooldown on scale.
\item \emph{Shrinking gains.} Why do the gains of Muon, SOAP and other matrix methods over AdamW shrink with model size, and do methods that fix the weight norms directly, such as Hyperball \parencite{WenEtAl2026Hyperball}, keep them (\cref{ch:spectral,ch:kronecker,ch:decay})?
\item \emph{Hyperparameter scaling laws.} How should the learning rate, the batch size and the weight decay scale with model size and data size together? The book gives the exponent of width (\cref{ch:modular}), the critical batch size of a quadratic (\cref{ch:noise}) and the timescale of weight decay (\cref{ch:decay}); a joint theory is open.
\item \emph{Spikes.} A solvable model of a loss spike in a transformer, with the optimizer state, the data and the architecture all playing their parts, does not exist (\cref{ch:spikes}).
\item \emph{Beyond pretraining.} The optimizers of this book are tuned for pretraining. Their behavior in mixtures of experts, in reinforcement learning from feedback, in low precision and in fine-tuning raises questions that the book does not touch.
\end{enumerate}

\addsec{How to turn a practice into a theorem}

The chapters followed one method, and it may be useful to state it. Begin with the practice as practitioners state it, with its numbers and its sources, and with the evidence for and against it; the first section of every chapter is an attempt at this, and the attempt often showed that the practice was folklore. Find the smallest idealization in which the practice has a reason: one coordinate for sign descent, one matrix for Muon, a cubic for the edge of stability, a valley with a river for schedules, a sphere for weight decay. Prove what the idealization implies, completely, so that its consequences are not in doubt. Extract a number from the proof: a threshold, an exponent, a rate, a timescale, an equilibrium. Compare the number with a simulation, and then with training runs, and report the disagreement. Finally, list what the idealization threw away, and look in that list for the next idealization.

The method has a limit that the ledger makes plain: it explains mechanisms one at a time, in models that are much smaller than the networks they describe. Whether the mechanisms add up to an account of large-scale training is the question this book leaves for the next one.
